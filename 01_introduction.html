1. Course Objectives

After completing this course, you should be able to:

  • Develop a basic understanding of the Finite Element Method (FEM) and its engineering applications, building on Matrix Algebra, Statics, and Mechanics of Materials.
  • Understand the assumptions FEM makes when analyzing a physical system.
  • Formulate finite element equations for 1‑D, 2‑D, and 3‑D problems.
  • Apply the mathematics used throughout the FEA process.
  • Formulate and solve structural problems using commercial software such as ABAQUS.
  • Make and document appropriate assumptions, select proper elements, and develop finite element models that adequately and efficiently represent physical systems.
  • Correctly interpret results and use them to guide design changes to a component or subsystem.
  • Recognize the risks and limitations of FE solutions and simulations.
  • Be prepared for further study, research, and development in finite element techniques.

3. From Physics to Solutions in Engineering Problems

In the vast majority of engineering problems, complex geometries and material behavior are encountered. Closed‑form or analytical solutions of the governing differential equations are extremely difficult to obtain, and very often cannot be found at all.

The finite element method can analyze linear and nonlinear problems with complex geometries, material behavior, and boundary conditions. It fits into the broader modeling pipeline like this:

Stage Examples
Physics laws Equilibrium, Hooke’s Law, Plasticity Law
Mathematical model Algebraic equations, differential equations, integral equations
Analytical solution Closed‑form solutions
Numerical solution Finite elements, finite differences, finite volume, boundary elements

FEM is one route — usually the most versatile one — from a mathematical model to a usable numerical solution when a closed‑form answer isn’t available.

4. History of the Finite Element Method

Adapted from Gander and Wanner (2004), “From Euler, Ritz, and Galerkin to Modern Computing.”

The modern FEM emerged from nearly a century of work on approximating solutions to physical problems, long before digital computers existed.

The development of the Finite Element Method (FEM) is a rich historical narrative bridging acoustic physics, variational calculus, weighted residual mathematics, and localized geometry. It began in 1787 when Ernst Chladni discovered that exciting a metal plate with a violin bow created intricate sand patterns corresponding to different vibration frequencies. Modeled mathematically by Gustav Kirchhoff in 1850 as a fourth-order biharmonic eigenvalue problem (\(\Delta^2 w = \lambda w\)), the equations for free-edged plates resisted exact analytical solutions for decades.

To crack this problem, Walther Ritz published a groundbreaking paper in 1909. Rather than solving the complex differential equations directly, Ritz worked from the principle of least action, approximating continuous plate displacement (\(w\)) as a finite linear combination of global coordinate functions. This reduced an infinite-dimensional problem into a discrete matrix eigenvalue problem (\(Ka = \lambda a\)), yielding stunning accuracy against Chladni's physical experiments and proving the practical power of direct variational methods.

While Ritz laid the variational foundation, the Russian school—specifically Ivan Bubnov and Boris Galerkin—provided the mathematical breakthrough that freed the method from its strict reliance on energy minimization. In 1913, structural engineer Ivan Bubnov observed that an energy functional was not strictly necessary; instead, approximations could be substituted directly into the governing equilibrium equations, multiplied by a coordinate function, and integrated over the volume to generate a system of linear equations. Boris Galerkin formalized and generalized this in 1915 by proving that coordinate functions did not need to be orthogonal—introducing the mass matrix—and showing that the approach worked even when no underlying variational principle existed. This generalization expanded FEM far beyond elasticity into fluid dynamics and heat transfer.

Despite these advancements, a major geometric bottleneck remained: finding suitable global coordinate functions for irregularly shaped objects was exceptionally difficult. To overcome this, Richard Courant proposed a revolutionary modification in his 1941 address: replacing global functions with localized piecewise linear "hat functions" defined over an arbitrarily chosen triangular mesh. This transition marked the formal birth of spatial discretization in FEM.

The technique finally received its official name in 1960 at the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) Conference in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, where engineer Ray Clough presented his landmark paper on plane stress analysis. Although Clough and Jon Turner of Boeing had published the first mathematical description of the method in 1956, Clough's 1960 presentation officially christened it the "finite element method." Today, the modern approach—almost universally implemented as the Galerkin Finite Element Method—combines Ritz's energy concepts, Bubnov and Galerkin's weighted residual framework, and Courant's localized meshes to simulate everything from structural mechanics to thermal transfer.

Year Contributor Contribution

1909 Walther Ritz Introduced the Ritz method: an approximate solution built from a sum of trial (basis) functions, chosen to minimize a system’s energy
1913 Ivan Bubnov Proposed a related weighted‑residual approach, now paired with Galerkin’s name
1915 Boris Galerkin Formalized the Bubnov–Galerkin method for turning differential equations into algebraic systems using weighted residuals
1943 Richard Courant Applied piecewise polynomial (“hat”) trial functions defined over small subdomains — element‑by‑element — foreshadowing the modern finite element

From name to discipline. After Clough’s 1960 talk, engineers spent the early 1960s applying the method to stress analysis, fluid flow, heat transfer, and other problems. Zienkiewicz and Cheung published the first FEM textbook in 1967, and by the early 1970s FEM was in use across a wide range of engineering disciplines.

Takeaway: each contributor solved the same underlying problem — how to approximate a continuous field with a finite set of numbers — with an increasingly practical choice of trial function: global polynomials (Ritz) → trigonometric/weighted‑residual functions (Bubnov–Galerkin) → local, element‑wise “hat” functions (Courant). That last step, localizing the approximation to small subdomains, is the conceptual birth of the finite element.

5. FEM in Practice

The two examples below illustrate the range of problems FEM is used for today, from complex multi‑part assemblies to fine surface meshes on curved geometry.

Automotive crash safety. Manufacturers mesh an entire vehicle body-in-white — thousands of stamped panels, welds, and bolted joints — and run nonlinear, large-deformation FEM simulations of frontal, side, and rollover impacts before a single physical prototype is crash-tested. The mesh density is highest around crumple zones and occupant compartments, where the deformation and stress gradients are largest.

Civil infrastructure. Bridge and building designers use FEM to check how a structure responds to wind, seismic loading, and thermal expansion, combining beam, shell, and solid elements in the same model to represent girders, decks, and foundations at appropriate levels of detail.

6. What Is the Finite Element Method?

  • Engineering definition: FEM is a numerical procedure for the analysis of physical systems.
  • Mathematical definition: FEM is a numerical procedure for finding approximate solutions of systems of partial differential equations.

FEM research and development spans many disciplines, including engineering, mathematics, and computer science.

Overview. FEM is the method of analysis in which the field equations of mathematical physics are approximated over simple regions (triangles, quadrilaterals, tetrahedra, etc.), which are then assembled together so that equilibrium or continuity is satisfied at the interconnecting nodal points of the domains.

7. Basic Concepts

FEM rests on three core ideas: nodes, degrees of freedom, and elements.

Nodes A structure or domain is discretized into a finite number of points called nodes. They are the primary locations where unknown field variables (displacement, temperature, pressure, etc.) are calculated. The behavior of the entire structure is approximated by interpolating values between nodes within each element.

Degrees of Freedom (DOF) Degrees of freedom represent the independent ways a system can move or deform. At each node, there are specific DOFs corresponding to the physical variables being analyzed. For structural analysis, these typically include translations (along X, Y, Z) and rotations (about X, Y, Z). The number of DOFs at a node depends on the element type and the problem’s dimensionality. In general, 2D elements have 2 DOF/node (translations along X and Y), while 3D elements have 3 DOF/node (translations along X, Y, and Z); additional rotational DOFs are introduced for elements such as beams, plates, and shells.

A simple 2D truss with 5 nodes and only translational DOFs would have 5 × 2 = 10 DOFs total; supports and hinges reduce that number by constraining some of them.

Elements Finite elements are the small, discrete parts into which the continuous domain is divided; they connect to each other at shared nodes. Common element types include:

  • 1‑D elements (line/truss elements) — for slender structures like beams or trusses; nodes at the ends.
  • 2‑D elements (triangular, quadrilateral) — for thin plates, shells, or planar domains; nodes at the corners (and sometimes mid‑sides).
  • 3‑D elements (tetrahedral, hexahedral/brick) — for solid structures; nodes at the corners of the 3‑D shape.

Each element has its own set of equations relating its nodal DOFs to the forces or loads applied to it. These element equations are then assembled into a global system of equations for the entire structure.

8. The Finite Element Analysis Workflow

The seven steps below form a single, continuous process — each step’s output is the next step’s input. Picture a simple cantilever beam being carried through all seven stages: mesh it, approximate the deflection within each element, build each element’s stiffness relation, assemble them into one global system, apply the fixed support and tip load, solve for the unknown deflections, then recover stress and strain from those deflections.

Steps 2–4 below are exactly where the mathematical tools developed in Lectures 02–05 get used: vectors and matrices (Lecture 02) give the notation for element and global stiffness relations; interpolation (Lecture 03) is how shape functions are built; and differentiation and integration (Lecture 05) are how strains are recovered from displacement fields and how element matrices are evaluated.

  1. Discretization of the continuum — Divide the object into a finite number of elements connected at nodes. The full collection of elements and nodes is called the finite element mesh.

  2. Selection of interpolation functions — Assume a simple function (usually a polynomial) to approximate the unknown field variable (e.g., displacement) within each element. This function is defined by the nodal values; the choice of function directly determines the accuracy of the solution.

  3. Formulation of element equations — For each element, derive the element stiffness matrix, \([K^e]\), relating nodal forces \(\{F^e\}\) to nodal displacements \(\{u^e\}\) via the element’s material properties and geometry. This step results in one equation per element:

    \[[K^e]\{u^e\}=\{F^e\}\]

  4. Assembly of global equations — Combine the individual element stiffness matrices into one large system for the entire structure:

    \[[K]\{u\}=\{F\}\]

    where [K] is the global stiffness matrix, {u} the global displacement vector, and {F} the global (total) force vector.

  5. Application of boundary conditions — Apply constraints and external loads to the global system. This simulates real supports (fixed or pinned ends, etc.) and makes the system solvable.

  6. Solution for unknowns — Solve the assembled system of linear equations for the unknown nodal displacements. This is typically the most computationally intensive part of the process, and is performed by a computer.

  7. Computation of derived variables — Use the calculated nodal displacements to determine other quantities of interest, such as strain and stress.

9. Advantages and Disadvantages of FEM

Advantages

  • Handles a wide variety of engineering problems: solid mechanics, dynamics, fluid mechanics, heat transfer, electrostatics.
  • Readily handles very complex geometry and complex constraints.
  • Handles complex material properties (linear, nonlinear, time‑dependent, …).
  • Handles complex loading.

Disadvantages

  • FEM produces only approximate numerical solutions.
  • It does not produce a general closed‑form solution that would let you examine system response to parameter changes analytically.
  • FEM has inherent (discretization, numerical) errors.
  • Mistakes by users can be fatal — qualified users are required. In student work specifically, poor mesh quality (badly shaped or overly coarse elements) and incorrectly specified boundary conditions are the two most common sources of wrong answers — far more often than an error in the underlying theory.

10. Commercial Finite Element Software

Common commercial FE packages include:

  • ANSYS
  • ABAQUS
  • ADINA
  • COMSOL Multiphysics
  • CosmosWorks (part of SolidWorks)
  • …and others

Open-source alternatives have matured enough for serious use as well — FEniCS, CalculiX, and Code_Aster are widely used in research and industry, and are worth knowing even if the course itself is taught in ABAQUS.

Typical components of commercial software:

  • Pre‑processor — used to build the finite element model.
  • Solver — conducts the numerical analysis.
  • Post‑processor — displays the calculation results graphically.

11. Course Roadmap

This course builds from fundamental mathematical concepts to full 3‑D nonlinear finite element analysis using commercial software.

The first part of the course focuses on the mathematical foundations needed to master FEM, including vectors and matrices, interpolation, numerical integration, and the basics of programming in Python. No prior programming experience is required.

Next, you will be introduced to the stiffness method. We will begin with simple 1‑D spring systems and gradually progress to 2‑D truss and frame structures. As a practical example, you will analyze the Smithfield Street Bridge.

This will be followed by an introduction to energy and Ritz methods. We will derive and study various 2‑D and 3‑D finite elements, including bilinear, quadratic, and isoparametric elements. We will also discuss important practical aspects of FEM modeling, including element type and size selection, convergence, verification and validation, and common modeling mistakes.

Finally, you will apply the concepts learned throughout the course to analyze a variety of structures using ABAQUS.
