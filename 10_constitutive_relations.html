Lecture 6

Hooke's Law in Three Dimensions

Every solid element in your FE model carries a small piece of material physics wired into it: the constitutive matrix [D]. It's what turns strain into stress inside Ke = ∫ Bᵗ D B dV. This page builds up to [D] from the ground up — starting from what a stress and a strain component actually are, through σ = Eε, adding Poisson's effect, generalizing to 3‑D, then writing the whole thing in the vector/matrix (Voigt) notation your FE code actually uses — with a live panel where you can watch [D] change as you change E and ν.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  1. Define a stress or strain component from its two subscripts (face and direction), and distinguish normal components from shear components.
  2. State Hooke's law for a 1-D bar and extend it — via superposition and Poisson's effect — to the full 3-D stress–strain relationship.
  3. Assemble the 3-D constitutive matrix [D] in vector/matrix (Voigt) notation relating {σ} to {ε}, and identify the roles of E, ν, and G within it.
  4. Invert the compliance relationship to write {σ} = [D]{ε}, and explain why an isotropic [D] is fully determined by just two independent elastic constants.
  5. Explain why [D] is the same matrix regardless of which coordinate axes are chosen, for an isotropic material.
  6. Distinguish the plane-stress and plane-strain assumptions, derive the corresponding reduced [D] matrices, and identify why plane-strain elements are prone to volumetric locking as ν → 0.5.
  7. Use the live [D] calculator to predict how changing E and ν affects the stiffness terms and the resulting strains or stresses for a given loading.
1-D: σ = Eε → + Poisson's ν → 3-D strain-from-stress → invert → stress-from-strain → Voigt [D] matrix → 2-D reductions

Each arrow is a "why" you'll be able to answer by the end of the corresponding section: Sections 3–4 build σ = Eε for a single direction; Section 5 adds the Poisson coupling between directions; Section 6 stacks all three directions into {ε} from {σ}; Section 9 inverts that relationship to get {σ} from {ε}; Section 8 packages the result as the matrix [D] your FE code actually stores; and Section 11 shows the two ways [D] shrinks from 6×6 to 3×3 for 2-D elements. If any one link in this chain feels shaky later in the course, that's the section to come back to.

small strain  +  linear elasticity  +  isotropy  +  homogeneity

  • Small strain: displacement gradients are small enough that the linearized strain–displacement relations (Section 2.0) apply — no large rotations or large stretches.
  • Linear elasticity: stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law), and unloading is fully recoverable — no yielding, cracking, or permanent set.
  • Isotropy: the material responds identically in every direction, which is what lets a single [D] built from just E and ν describe it, unchanged under rotation (Section 10.0).
  • Homogeneity: E and ν are constant within a given element or material region, unless a problem explicitly states otherwise.
  • No couple stresses or body moments: this is what makes the stress tensor symmetric (τij = τji), used throughout.

Keep this list in mind as a checklist: most of the "gotchas" on this page (a wrong shear factor, a wrong plane-stress/strain choice, a surprising [D] transformation) come from quietly violating one of these five assumptions, not from a new physical effect.

⚠️
What this model cannot do.

Linear isotropic elasticity is a deliberately narrow model. It does not capture yielding, cracking, damage, plasticity, creep, viscoelasticity, or any direction-dependent (anisotropic) material behavior — those need different constitutive laws entirely, not just a different E and ν. Small-strain kinematics become unreliable once rotations or stretches get large, regardless of how elastic the material is. Plane stress and plane strain are idealizations of the through-thickness behavior, not automatic consequences of a part being thin or long — see the caution in Section 11.0's self-check. And nearly incompressible materials (ν → 0.5) need special numerical treatment — reduced integration or a mixed formulation — in plane strain and 3-D elements, or the element locks (Section 7.0).

None of that makes this page's material wrong for the course — it's exactly the right starting model — but it's worth knowing where the edges are before you reach for it on a problem outside them.

Common mistakes this page is designed to prevent:
  • Mixing up the τ subscript order (face vs. direction) — §1.0
  • Confusing engineering vs. tensor shear strain (γ vs. e) — §2.0 / §8.0
  • Picking the wrong plane-stress/plane-strain reduction — §11.0
  • Missing volumetric locking as ν → 0.5 — §7.0 / §11.0
1.0

Defining stress: a magnitude, a face, and a direction

A stress is a force divided by the area it acts on — but on a 3-D element, that's not quite enough information. A single number can't capture which internal surface the force is pushing on, or which way it's pushing. So every stress component carries two pieces of directional information, and that's why it needs two subscripts: τij, where the first subscript is the direction of the face's outward normal, and the second is the direction the stress itself points.

When the two subscripts match (the stress points perpendicular to its own face), it's a normal stress, and convention swaps the letter to σ: σx is shorthand for τxx. When the subscripts differ, the stress lies in the plane of the face — a shear stress, τij with i≠j.

Sign convention: every cube diagram and equation on this page uses the standard continuum-mechanics convention — tension positive, compression negative — for normal stresses and strains. If you're coming from beam theory, where sagging/hogging or top-fiber/bottom-fiber conventions are common, don't carry those over here: check that "positive" means tensile before plugging a beam-theory number into anything on this page.
2.0

Defining strain: stretching and distorting

Strain describes deformation, not force — but it splits into the same two flavors as stress. An extensional (normal) strain is a change in length per unit original length along one axis. A shear strain is a change in angle between two lines that started out perpendicular.

More rigorously: strain is a derivative of displacement. Every point in the body moves by some displacement vector — call its components (ux, uy, uz), functions of position. ΔL/L and α+β above are really just the average, finite-difference version of a simpler idea: strain measures how fast displacement changes from one point to its neighbor. That's a spatial derivative.

Each piece matches something already on the page. A normal strain ex = ∂ux/∂x is just ΔLx/Lx written for an infinitesimal segment instead of the whole bar. And the two rotations from the shear figure above are individual derivative terms: β = ∂ux/∂y is how much the x-displacement changes as you move up in y — that's exactly what tips the originally-vertical edge over — while α = ∂uy/∂x is how much the y-displacement changes as you move along x, tipping the originally-horizontal edge. Add them for the engineering shear strain γxy = α + β = ∂ux/∂y + ∂uy/∂x, or average them for the tensor value exy — same α and β as before, just written as derivatives.

You'll need the partial-derivative mechanics from Differentiation and Integration to move comfortably between the two forms above (ΔL/L vs. ∂u/∂x) — every strain–displacement relationship in Lecture 07's 3-D continuum formulation is written this same derivative way, so it's worth being fluent in it now rather than re-deriving it there.

Why this matters for FEM: this derivative relationship, {ε} = [strain-displacement operator]{u}, is exactly what the [B] matrix encodes in Section 8.0's Ke = ∫Bᵗ D B dV. An FE element doesn't know the true displacement field — it only has nodal displacements and shape functions. [B] is just this same set of partial derivatives, applied to the shape functions instead of an exact u(x,y,z), to turn nodal displacements into strain at any point in the element.
The fine print: everything above is linearized — it assumes the displacement gradients (and the rotations α, β) are small enough that higher-order terms can be dropped. That's an excellent approximation for the stiff, lightly-strained structures most of Sections 3.0–11.0 are built around. Push into large rotations or large stretches — rubber, fabric, metal forming — and these simple partial-derivative expressions stop holding; you need finite-strain measures (Green–Lagrange, Almansi, logarithmic strain) that keep the quadratic terms this page has been quietly discarding.
Which one goes in the code: despite the tensor value e being the "proper" strain, most FE codes pack the engineering shear strain γ, not e, into the Voigt {ε} vector. The reason is virtual work: strain energy is computed as a sum of stress × conjugate strain terms, and γ is the strain that pairs directly with shear stress τ in that product without an extra factor of 2 floating around. Section 8.0 builds that {ε} vector and the full 6×6 [D] matrix explicitly — keep this γ-vs-e distinction in mind once you get there.
3.0

What "elastic" actually means

A material is elastic if it responds to load instantly and returns exactly to its original shape once the load is removed. That's the whole definition — nothing here requires the stress–strain curve to be a straight line.

Elastic can be linear or nonlinear. The only way to tell the difference between "elastic" and "permanently deformed" is to look at the unloading path. If unloading retraces the loading path back to the origin, it's elastic — linear if that path is straight, nonlinear if it's curved.

Everything below assumes linear elastic behavior — the straight-line case, where a single constant of proportionality connects stress and strain.
4.0

The one-dimensional starting point

Pull on a bar along its axis and, for a linear elastic material, the axial stress is directly proportional to the axial strain:

σa  =  E · εa

The proportionality constant E is the modulus of elasticity (Young's modulus) — a material property, not a geometry property. It's the slope of the linear elastic line in the plots above, and it varies enormously between materials:

6.0

Going 3-D: stack the stresses one at a time

A real element in a structure carries normal stress in all three directions at once: σx, σy, σz. Because the material is linear elastic, we can find the combined effect by superposition — work out the strain caused by each stress acting alone, then add the results.

Apply σx by itself. It stretches the element along x by σx/E, and — through Poisson's effect — squeezes it by the same fraction in both of the other directions, because the material is isotropic (it behaves the same regardless of direction):

σx ACTING ALONE

ex = σx/E
ey = −ν·σx/E
ez = −ν·σx/E

σy ACTING ALONE

ey = σy/E
ex = −ν·σy/E
ez = −ν·σy/E

σz ACTING ALONE

ez = σz/E
ex = −ν·σz/E
ey = −ν·σz/E
ex = σx/E − ν·σy/E − ν·σz/E
ey = σy/E − ν·σx/E − ν·σz/E
ez = σz/E − ν·σx/E − ν·σy/E

Nothing applied yet — click "Step 1" to bring in σx's contribution to all three strains, then add σy and σz one at a time.

ex  =  (1/E) · [ σx  −  ν(σy + σz) ]
ey  =  (1/E) · [ σy  −  ν(σx + σz) ]
ez  =  (1/E) · [ σz  −  ν(σx + σy) ]

Read each line the same way: your own stress stretches you; the other two squeeze you. Only two material constants — E and ν — are needed for the entire 3-D response of an isotropic linear elastic solid.

⚠️
Near-incompressible warning — this breaks standard elements.

As ν approaches 0.5, the material becomes nearly incompressible — it strongly resists any change in volume, only reshaping. The (1−2ν) term sitting in the denominator of the scale factor above tends to zero, so the scale factor (and every entry of [D] built from it) blows up toward infinity. A standard displacement-based element enforces that near-zero volume change pointwise, at every integration point inside the element — which is a much stricter constraint than the element's displacement field actually has the freedom to satisfy. The element ends up with too few displacement modes to accommodate an (almost) volume-preserving deformation, so it fights itself and goes artificially stiff — a numerical artifact called locking, not a real material property.

The two standard fixes both loosen that pointwise constraint rather than tightening the element: reduced integration checks the volume constraint at fewer Gauss points, so the element has more freedom to deform nearly-incompressibly without being penalized at every location; a mixed u–p formulation goes further and treats pressure as its own independently-interpolated field (rather than deriving it from the displacement field), enforcing incompressibility weakly instead of pointwise. Either way, the fix works around the same root cause: plain displacement elements have no slack for volume-preserving deformation once (1−2ν) gets small.

The checkerboard on the right is the classic visual signature of volumetric locking — a spurious, mesh-dependent oscillation in the computed pressure/stress field, alternating element-to-element even though the applied load and geometry are perfectly smooth. Seeing that pattern in your own results is a strong hint you need reduced integration or a mixed formulation, not a finer mesh.

8.0

Rewriting it as a matrix: the form FEM actually uses

Section 6.0's three equations only cover normal stress and strain. A general 3-D state also has shear. FE codes stack all six stress components and all six strain components into column vectors — Voigt notation — so the whole constitutive law becomes one matrix equation instead of six separate ones. Shear strains here are the engineering shear strains, γ = 2e (double the tensor shear strain, for the symmetry reason explained in Section 2.0's callout), which is the convention Voigt notation requires:

The matrix-vector multiply {σ} = [D]{ε} you're about to build is exactly the operation covered in Lecture 02 — Vectors & Matrices for FEM: a 6×6 matrix times a 6×1 vector, row by row. The two describe the same physical distortion — γ is always exactly twice e. The Voigt vector {ε} above, the matrix multiply {σ} = [D]{ε} in Section 9.0, and every live calculator on this page (Sections 7.0 and 11.0) all use engineering shear strain (γ). If you ever build a [B] matrix or a strain tensor by hand, watch for that factor of 2 — plugging tensor e into a Voigt {ε} silently halves your computed shear stiffness. 3-D Continuum Formulation assumes engineering shear throughout its {ε} = [B]{de} derivation, so this same mixup there shows up as a wrong [B] matrix and a wrong strain energy density — not only as a wrong [D].

Hooke's law then reads {σ} = [D]{ε}, where [D] is the constitutive matrix (or elasticity matrix / stiffness matrix in the material-property sense — not to be confused with the element stiffness matrix Ke that it feeds into).

A note on ordering: the component order used here — normal terms first (σx, σy, σz), then shear (τxy, τxz, τyz), with strain ordered to match — is a convention, not a law of nature, but it's a common one: it matches, for example, Abaqus's stress output order (S11, S22, S33, S12, S13, S23). Some references order shear differently (e.g. xy, yz, zx) or interleave normal and shear terms; if you ever read [D] out of a different source, check its component order before assuming the entries line up with the matrix below. This is also the ordering used later in this course's 3-D Continuum Formulation lecture, so its [B] matrix lines up directly with the [D] built here.

Progression to remember: anisotropic (21 constants) → orthotropic (9 constants, no normal/shear coupling) → isotropic (2 constants, identical in every direction). Each step adds material symmetry and removes independent constants. One practical consequence follows directly from that symmetry: an orthotropic [D] is defined in the material's own principal axes (e.g. along/across fiber directions in a composite ply), so before it can be assembled into Ke it has to be transformed into the element's global or local coordinate frame — get that orientation wrong and the whole element responds along the wrong directions. An isotropic [D] never needs this step; because it's identical in every direction by definition, there's no "wrong orientation" to worry about, which is one more reason the isotropic case is the default starting point. The rest of this page — and every element you build in this course — uses the isotropic case. Worked example — full 6×6 [D], structural steel

Take structural steel, E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.3, and a strain state {ε} = {0.0010, 0.0005, −0.0003, 0.0008, 0, 0} (engineering shear strain γxy in the fourth slot). First the scale factor and the two distinct entry values:

scale = E/[(1+ν)(1−2ν)] = 200,000/(1.3 × 0.4) = 384,615 MPa
diagonal (normal) = scale·(1−ν) = 269,231 MPa    off-diagonal (normal) = scale·ν = 115,385 MPa
diagonal (shear) = scale·(1−2ν)/2 = 76,923 MPa  (= G, matches E/[2(1+ν)])

Multiplying {ε} through the top-left 3×3 block and the shear diagonal:

σx = 269,231(0.0010) + 115,385(0.0005) + 115,385(−0.0003) = 292.3 MPa
σy = 115,385(0.0010) + 269,231(0.0005) + 115,385(−0.0003) = 215.4 MPa

Every entry came from just two numbers, E and ν — the isotropy payoff from Section 8.0's constant-count table.

9.0

Flipping it around: stress in terms of strain

Section 6.0 gives strain as a function of stress. Sometimes — especially in finite element formulations — you need the inverse: stress as a function of strain. Add the three strain equations together and the shear-inducing cross terms cancel neatly, leaving a relationship between the volume change (ex+ey+ez) and the total normal stress:

[ E / (1 − 2ν) ] · ( ex + ey + ez )  =  σx + σy + σz

Substituting that back into the original equation for σx (and doing the same for σy, σz) isolates each normal stress in terms of strains alone:

σx = 2μ·ex + λ·(ex + ey + ez)
σy = 2μ·ey + λ·(ex + ey + ez)
σz = 2μ·ez + λ·(ex + ey + ez)

where μ and λ are the Lamé parameters — just E and ν repackaged:

μ = E / [2(1+ν)]
λ = νE / [(1−2ν)(1+ν)]

μ is actually a familiar quantity in disguise: it's the shear modulus, G — the same constant that relates shear stress to shear strain, τ = Gγ. So the same two independent constants, E and ν, govern normal response, volumetric response, and shear response all at once, for an isotropic linear elastic material.

One more repackaging worth having on hand is the bulk modulus, K — the constant relating a uniform (hydrostatic) pressure to the resulting volume change, p = −K·(ΔV/V):

K = E / [3(1−2ν)]

Between the three of them — G (shear response), λ (a Lamé parameter with no standalone physical picture, mostly useful for the compact 2μe+λ(Σe) form above), and K (bulk/volumetric response) — every one is just E and ν reparameterized, never a third or fourth independent material property. K makes the incompressible limit concrete: as ν → 0.5, the (1−2ν) in its denominator shrinks toward zero and K → ∞ — the material resists any volume change at all, which is exactly the near-incompressible locking behavior flagged in Section 7.0's live calculator.

Worked example — same steel, via λ and μ

Same steel as Section 8.0's example, E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.3:

μ = E/[2(1+ν)] = 200,000/2.6 = 76,923 MPa  (matches G above)
λ = νE/[(1−2ν)(1+ν)] = (0.3)(200,000)/(0.4 × 1.3) = 115,385 MPa

Reusing ex = 0.0010, ey = 0.0005, ez = −0.0003 from Section 8.0, the volumetric term is (ex+ey+ez) = 0.0012:

σx = 2μ·ex + λ·0.0012 = 2(76,923)(0.0010) + 115,385(0.0012) = 292.3 MPa

Same 292.3 MPa either way — the 6×6 matrix multiply in Section 8.0 and the Lamé form here are just two ways of writing the identical isotropic law.

10.0

One more coordinate system, same [D]

Careful with the wording here: it's the constitutive law that's rotationally invariant, not the matrix taken literally as a fixed grid of numbers. A specific [D] is always written for stress and strain components expressed in some chosen coordinate system. Rotate the axes, and both {σ} and {ε} pick up new components — but because the material is isotropic, the relationship {σ} = [D]{ε} holds with exactly the same numbers in [D] no matter which axes you picked to write it in. That's a much stronger statement than "many materials look the same in different directions"; it's the specific reason a single [D], computed once from E and ν, gets reused unchanged for every element in a mesh — each element just plugs in strains expressed in its own local axes, and the same [D] gives back the correct stresses in those same axes.

One concrete place this shows up: rotate into a frame where the shear stresses vanish (a state described by principal stresses σ₁ and σ₂, with the third principal stress zero). The strain tensor comes out equally simple, with off-diagonal shear-strain terms equal to zero too:

e11 = (σ₁ − ν·σ₂) / E
e22 = (σ₂ − ν·σ₁) / E

Same structure, same two constants, same [D] — because that's all the material law feeding your stiffness matrix ever needed, and it's unchanged whether x/y or the principal directions 1/2 are what you call your axes.

The example above sets the third principal stress to zero only to keep it short — with all three principal stresses nonzero, Section 6.0's three equations apply completely unchanged: e11 = [σ₁ − ν(σ₂+σ₃)]/E, and similarly for e22 and e33. Rotating to principal axes never changes which equations you use, only which axes you call x, y, and z.

Isn't this just a coincidence of the numbers? No — and this is not true for an anisotropic material. For wood or a fiber-reinforced composite, [D] genuinely changes when you rotate axes. That transformation is more involved than a single generic rotation matrix applied the same way on both sides: because Voigt notation packs the strain vector with engineering shear (γ, not the tensor e = γ/2), stress and strain don't transform with the identical rule, so the constitutive matrix must be transformed consistently with whichever convention you're using — not with one shared [R] naively sandwiched around [D]. This course focuses on the isotropic case, for which no such transformation is ever needed: isotropy is precisely the special property that makes [D] come back out unchanged under rotation. That's why this page only ever needs one [D] per material, while an orthotropic material needs its [D] recomputed (or properly transformed) for every distinct element orientation — a topic for a materials or advanced FEM course, not this one.
11.0

Going 2-D: plane stress and plane strain

Section 8.0's [D] is a full 3-D, 6×6 matrix. Most 2-D elements never use it — they use one of two 3×3 reductions, and the two are not interchangeable. Each comes from a different physical assumption about what's happening in the out-of-plane (z) direction, so picking the wrong one for a given geometry is one of the more common silent modeling errors in FEA.

PLANE STRESS  — assume σz = τzx = τzy = 0

Definition: the out-of-plane normal and shear stresses are taken to be zero everywhere. This holds for thin plates and shells loaded only in their own plane — there's no material out-of-plane to react against a z-stress, so none can build up. ez is not zero; it's free to change as the plate thins or thickens under the in-plane loads (that's Poisson's effect from Section 5.0, still happening, just not suppressed): setting σz = 0 in Section 6.0's third equation gives ez = −(ν/E)(σx + σy) — nonzero whenever σx or σy is. "2-D" means σz = 0, not ez = 0.

PLANE STRAIN  — assume ez = γzx = γzy = 0

Definition: the out-of-plane normal and shear strains are taken to be zero everywhere. This holds for long prismatic bodies restrained along their length — dams, tunnels, long shafts — where material further along z physically prevents that cross-section from changing length in z. σz is not zero here; it builds up to whatever value is needed to keep ez at zero.

Each assumption, substituted into the full 3-D law from Section 8.0, collapses the 6×6 matrix down to the 3×3 that only needs {σx, σy, τxy} and {ex, ey, γxy}:

Check your understanding

Three quick prompts pulling from different sections above. Try each one before revealing the answer.

1. (§1.0/§2.0) A strain gauge reads γxy = 0.006 directly off a Mohr's-circle-style readout. What's the corresponding tensor shear strain exy, and which one would you plug into the Voigt {ε} vector from Section 8.0?

exy = γxy/2 = 0.003. But the Voigt {ε} vector wants γxy = 0.006, the engineering value straight off the gauge — no halving needed for that step.

2. (§5.0/§6.0) For steel (E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.3) under σx = 100 MPa alone (σy = σz = 0), is ey positive or negative, and roughly how big?

Negative — the material contracts laterally under axial tension. ey = −ν·σx/E = −0.3(100)/200,000 = −0.00015.

3. (§11.0) You're modeling a long, axially-restrained pressure-vessel wall, far from either end. Plane stress or plane strain — and what physically justifies that choice?

Plane strain. Far from the ends, the surrounding material prevents that cross-section from changing length in z (εz = 0), which is exactly the plane-strain assumption — not plane stress, which instead requires σz = 0 (true only near a free edge or in a thin, unrestrained plate).

Careful — this isn't automatic. The word axially-restrained is doing all the work in that setup, not the word "long." A long cylinder is not automatically plane strain: closed ends, an imposed axial load, or a free axial end all violate εz = 0 and change the reduction you should use. Always check the actual axial loading and restraint before defaulting to plane strain, not just the aspect ratio of the part. A good practical test: ask whether removing the surrounding material would let that cross-section move freely in z — if yes, the part isn't behaving as plane strain there, no matter how long or slender it looks.
