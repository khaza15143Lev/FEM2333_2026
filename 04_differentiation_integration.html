1. What is Differentiation?

  • Definition: Differentiation is a mathematical process that finds the rate at which a function changes.
  • Purpose: It measures how a function's output value changes as its input value changes.
  • Derivative: The derivative of $f(x)$ at a point $x$ represents the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f(x)$ at that point.
  • Symbol: The derivative of a function $f(x)$ is denoted $f'(x)$ or $\dfrac{df}{dx}$.

Figure 1. The derivative $f'(x_0)$ is the slope of the tangent line at $x_0$ — the limit of secant-line slopes as the second point moves toward $x_0$.

2. Limit Definition of the Derivative

Formula:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

Explanation: The derivative is defined as the limit of the average rate of change of the function as the interval $h$ approaches zero. This is exactly what Figure 1 shows: the secant line through $(x, f(x))$ and $(x+h, f(x+h))$ rotates into the tangent line as $h \to 0$.

3. Engineering Application: Deformation of a Rod

Introduce nodes 1 and 2 at the ends of a rod. In the undeformed state their coordinates are $x_1$ and $x_2$ respectively, with $x_2 > x_1$.

Initial (undeformed) length: $L = x_2 - x_1$

Figure 2. A rod under axial force, before (nodes at $x_1, x_2$) and after (outer markers, $x_1', x_2'$) deformation. Solid inner dots mark the original (undeformed) node positions $x_1,x_2$; open outer circles mark the deformed positions $x_1',x_2'$ after the rod stretches — $L$ is the original length, $L'$ the stretched length.

Displacement of node 1: $u_1 = x_1' - x_1$ Displacement of node 2: $u_2 = x_2' - x_2$

Length change: $$\Delta L = L' - L = (x_2' - x_1') - (x_2 - x_1) = u_2 - u_1 \equiv u(x+L) - u(x)$$

Concept

Strain measures how much a material stretches or shrinks relative to its original length — it is the length change per unit original length: $$\varepsilon = \frac{u(x+L) - u(x)}{L}$$

Procedure

As $L \to 0$, this becomes a derivative — strain is the derivative of displacement: $$\varepsilon(x) = \lim_{L \to 0} \frac{u(x+L) - u(x)}{L} = \frac{du}{dx}$$

FEM connection

This is the first of several places in FEM where a physical quantity (here, strain) turns out to be the derivative of another field (displacement). Every element formulation you build later computes strain this way, directly from the assumed displacement field.

Check

If $u(x)$ is constant, what is the strain? If $u(x) = cx$, what is the strain? (Answer: $0$, and $c$, respectively — a rigid shift produces no strain, and a linear field produces the constant strain $c$.)

4. Basic Differentiation Rules

Rule Formula
Power Rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}$
Sum Rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)+g(x)\big) = f'(x)+g'(x)$
Product Rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)g(x)\big) = f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)$
Quotient Rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right) = \dfrac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{g(x)^2}$
Chain Rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(f(g(x))\big) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$

Quick check for each rule:

  • Power: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3) = 3x^2$
  • Sum: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2 + x) = 2x + 1$
  • Product: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x \cdot \sin x) = \sin x + x\cos x$
  • Quotient: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{x}{x+1}\right) = \dfrac{1}{(x+1)^2}$
  • Chain: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big((x^2+1)^3\big) = 3(x^2+1)^2\cdot 2x = 6x(x^2+1)^2$

5. Worked Example: Differentiation

Given: $$y(x) = -5x^3 + 3x^2 + 6x - 5$$

Differentiate term by term using the power rule and sum rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} y'(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}(-5x^3) + \frac{d}{dx}(3x^2) + \frac{d}{dx}(6x) + \frac{d}{dx}(-5) \\[4pt] &= -5(3x^2) + 3(2x) + 6(1) + 0 \\[4pt] &= -15x^2 + 6x + 6 \end{aligned} $$

$$y'(x) = -15x^2 + 6x + 6$$

Differentiate $y(x) = -5x^3 + 3x^2 + 6x - 5$ term by term.

6. Partial Differentiation

  • Definition: For a function of more than one variable, e.g. $f(x,y)$, the partial derivative with respect to one variable is its ordinary derivative taken while every other variable is held fixed (treated as a constant).
  • Notation: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ (also written $f_x$) and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ (also written $f_y$).
  • Rule: Apply the same differentiation rules from Section 4 (power, sum, product, chain), treating every variable other than the one you are differentiating with respect to as a constant.

Collecting all partial derivatives of $f$ into a single vector gives the gradient: $$\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)$$

Worked Example

Given: $$f(x,y) = x^2y^2 + 3xy - 4y^3 + 7$$

Differentiate with respect to $x$, holding $y$ fixed (so $y^2$, $y$, and $-4y^3+7$ are constants): $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy^2 + 3y$$

Differentiate with respect to $y$, holding $x$ fixed: $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2x^2y + 3x - 12y^2$$

Find $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ for $f(x,y) = x^2y^2 + 3xy - 4y^3 + 7$.

Partial derivatives are what make multi-dimensional FEM possible: 2D and 3D strain components are partial derivatives of the displacement field (e.g. $\varepsilon_x=\partial u/\partial x$, $\gamma_{xy}=\partial u/\partial y+\partial v/\partial x$), and every entry of the isoparametric Jacobian matrix (Lecture 10) is a partial derivative of a physical coordinate with respect to a natural coordinate.

Check

For $f(x,y) = x^2y + 3y^2$, find $f_x$ and $f_y$. (Confirm you held the other variable fixed each time: $f_x = 2xy$, $f_y = x^2+6y$.)

7. What is Integration?

  • Definition: Integration is the reverse process of differentiation — it recovers a function from its rate of change.
  • Geometric meaning: The definite integral $\displaystyle\int_a^b y(x)\,dx$ is the signed area between the curve $y(x)$ and the $x$-axis, from $x=a$ to $x=b$.
  • Indefinite integral: $Y(x) = \int y(x)\,dx$ recovers a whole family of antiderivatives, differing by a constant $C$, since $\dfrac{d}{dx}(Y(x)+C) = y(x)$.

Figure 3. The definite integral $\int_a^b y(x)\,dx$ is the area under the curve between $x=a$ and $x=b$.

8. Indefinite Integrals

For a polynomial $y(x) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n} a_i x^i$:

$$Y(x) = \int y(x)\,dx = \sum_{i=0}^{n} a_i \frac{x^{i+1}}{i+1} + C$$

Example: $$y(x) = -5x^3 + 3x^2 + 6x - 5$$ $$\int y(x)\,dx = -\frac{5}{4}x^4 + x^3 + 3x^2 - 5x + C$$

Find $\int y(x)\,dx$ for $y(x) = -5x^3 + 3x^2 + 6x - 5$.

9. Definite Integrals

$$\int_a^b y(x)\,dx = \left(\sum_{i=0}^n a_i \frac{x^{i+1}}{i+1} + C\right)\Bigg|_a^b = \sum_{i=0}^n \frac{a_i}{i+1}\left(b^{i+1} - a^{i+1}\right)$$

Example: $$y(x) = -x^4 + 20x^2 + 6x + 5, \qquad \int_0^4 y(x)\,dx = \;?$$

Step by step:

$$ \int_0^4 y(x)\,dx = \left(-\frac{1}{5}x^5 + \frac{20}{3}x^3 + 3x^2 + 5x + C\right)\Bigg|_0^4 $$

$$ = \left(-\frac{1}{5}(4)^5 + \frac{20}{3}(4)^3 + 3(4)^2 + 5(4)\right) - \left(-\frac{1}{5}(0)^5 + \frac{20}{3}(0)^3 + 3(0)^2 + 5(0)\right) $$

$$\int_0^4 y(x)\,dx = 289\tfrac{13}{15} \approx 289.8667$$

This exact value is used below as the benchmark for every numerical method.

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^4 \big(-x^4+20x^2+6x+5\big)\,dx$.

10. Numerical Integration — Overview

Numerical integration approximates $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ when an exact antiderivative is unavailable or inconvenient (e.g., $f$ is only known at discrete points, as with finite element shape functions). The general strategy:

  1. Divide the interval of integration into sub-intervals.
  2. In each sub-interval, replace the true function with a simple function (e.g., a straight line or a parabola) that is easy to integrate exactly.
  3. Sum the integrals of the simple functions to approximate the exact integral.

Note: Accuracy depends on both the number of sub-intervals and the type of approximating function used. Throughout this section, $N$ denotes the number of sub-intervals (so there are $N+1$ evaluation points) — this convention is used consistently below to avoid ambiguity.

11. The Newton–Cotes Framework (general case)

The Trapezoidal rule is one member of a general family called the Newton–Cotes formulas: replace $f(x)$ on $[a,b]$ with the Lagrange polynomial $L(x)$ through $n+1$ equally spaced points, then integrate $L(x)$ exactly. (Fitting a higher-order polynomial through more equally spaced points, e.g. a parabola through three points, gives higher-order members of the same family — Simpson's rule is the best-known example — but this course focuses on the $n=1$ case below and on Gauss quadrature, which is the rule actually used in FEM.)

$$I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=0}^n w_i f(x_i), \qquad x_i = a + \frac{b-a}{n}i$$

$$w_i = \int_a^b L_{i,k}(x)\,dx = \int_a^b \prod_{\substack{j=0 \\ j\neq i}}^{n} \frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}\,dx$$

Figure 4. $n+1$ equally spaced points are used to build an interpolating polynomial $L(x)$; integrating $L(x)$ exactly gives the Newton–Cotes weights $w_i$.

Setting $n=1$ (a straight line through 2 points) gives the Trapezoidal Rule, derived below as a special case of this general framework.

12. Trapezoidal Rule ($n=1$ Newton–Cotes)

Approximate $f(x)$ by a straight line $g(x)$ through the endpoints, and integrate the straight line instead:

Figure 5. Trapezoidal rule: the curve is replaced by the chord connecting its endpoints.

$$I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \int_a^b g(x)\,dx = \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}(b-a)$$

Composite Trapezoidal Rule

For better accuracy, divide $[a,b]$ into $N$ equal sub-intervals of width $h = (b-a)/N$ and apply the rule on each one:

$$ I = \int_a^b y(x)\,dx = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} y(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \frac{y(x_i)+y(x_{i+1})}{2}\,h $$

Example: $y(x) = -x^4+20x^2+6x+5$ on $[0,4]$

With $N=8$ ($h=0.5$), the sub-intervals run from $x=0$ to $x=4$ (corrected — the original slide's sum erroneously continued one extra term out to $x=4.5$, which lies outside the integration domain):

$$ I \approx \frac{y(0)+y(0.5)}{2}(0.5) + \frac{y(0.5)+y(1)}{2}(0.5) + \cdots + \frac{y(3.5)+y(4)}{2}(0.5) $$

$N$ (sub-intervals) Points used $I$ Error
8 9 287.8750 $-0.69\%$
16 17 289.3672 $-0.17\%$

(Exact value: $289.8667$. As expected, doubling $N$ roughly quarters the error — Trapezoidal error decreases as $O(h^2)$.)

13. Gaussian Quadrature

Newton–Cotes formulas fix the evaluation points to be equally spaced and only choose the weights. Gauss's idea: also choose the locations of the points to maximize accuracy for a given number of evaluations.

$$I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i f(x_i)$$

where each $w_i$ is a weight and each $x_i$ is an integration point, both chosen (rather than fixed a priori) to make the rule exact for the highest possible polynomial degree: an $n$-point Gauss rule is exact for polynomials up to degree $2n-1$.

Mapping to the standard (parent) interval $[-1, 1]$

To make the rule independent of the integration limits, every Gauss rule is derived once on a fixed parent domain $[-1,1]$ and then mapped onto the physical interval $[a,b]$. Write the mapping in the reusable affine form used throughout FEM:

$$x(\xi) = \frac{1-\xi}{2}a + \frac{1+\xi}{2}b, \qquad \frac{dx}{d\xi} = \frac{b-a}{2} \equiv J$$

so that

$$I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_{-1}^{1} f\big(x(\xi)\big)\left|\frac{dx}{d\xi}\right|\,d\xi = J\int_{-1}^{1} f^*(\xi)\,d\xi \approx J\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\,f^*(\xi_i)$$

where $f^*(\xi) \equiv f(x(\xi))$. This "transform the integrand, then multiply by the Jacobian" pattern is the central idea of FEM element integration — it reappears unchanged in 2D and 3D. Four symbols are worth keeping straight:

  • $\xi_i$: a Gauss point, located in the parent domain $[-1,1]$.
  • $x_i = x(\xi_i)$: the corresponding point in the physical domain $[a,b]$.
  • $w_i$: the Gauss weight associated with $\xi_i$.
  • $J = dx/d\xi$: the mapping scale factor (the Jacobian), here a constant equal to $(b-a)/2$.

Deriving $n=1$

$$I = \int_{-1}^{1} f^*(\xi)\,d\xi \approx w_1 f^*(\xi_1)$$

Test with $f^*(\xi) = f^*(0) + \alpha \xi$ (the most general linear function):

$$\int_{-1}^{1}\big(f^*(0)+\alpha \xi\big)\,d\xi = \left(f^*(0)\,\xi + \alpha\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right)\Bigg|_{-1}^{1} = 2f^*(0) \quad\text{(independent of }\alpha\text{!)}$$

$$\boxed{w_1 = 2, \quad \xi_1 = 0}$$

Gaussian integration with $n=1$ is exact for any linear function — using only the midpoint.

Deriving $n=2$

Requiring the rule $I \approx w_1 f^*(\xi_1) + w_2 f^*(\xi_2)$ to be exact for $f^*(\xi) = \alpha_0+\alpha_1 \xi+\alpha_2 \xi^2+\alpha_3 \xi^3$ leads to the conditions:

$$w_1+w_2=2,\quad w_1\xi_1+w_2\xi_2=0,\quad w_1\xi_1^2+w_2\xi_2^2=\tfrac{2}{3},\quad w_1\xi_1^3+w_2\xi_2^3=0$$

Solving: $$\boxed{w_1=w_2=1, \quad \xi_1=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}, \quad \xi_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}}$$

$n=3$ (given without re-deriving; same procedure, exact through degree 5)

$$w_1=\tfrac{5}{9},\ \xi_1=-\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{5}}; \qquad w_2=\tfrac{8}{9},\ \xi_2=0; \qquad w_3=\tfrac{5}{9},\ \xi_3=\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{5}}$$

14. 2D Integration

Everything above integrates a function of a single variable over an interval $[a,b]$. FEM stiffness matrices for 2D elements (plane-stress/strain triangles, bilinear quadrilaterals, isoparametric elements — see Lectures 9–10) instead require integrating over an area $A$, so both the exact and numerical machinery need to be extended to two dimensions.

Double Integrals over a Rectangular Domain

For a region $A = [a,b]\times[c,d]$, the double integral of $f(x,y)$ is evaluated as two nested (iterated) single integrals — integrate with respect to one variable while holding the other fixed, then integrate the result with respect to the second variable:

$$I = \iint_A f(x,y)\,dA = \int_c^d\!\left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right) dy$$

Example: $f(x,y) = x^2y + xy^2$ on $A = [0,2]\times[0,1]$.

$$ \int_0^2 \big(x^2y+xy^2\big)\,dx = \left(\frac{x^3}{3}y + \frac{x^2}{2}y^2\right)\Bigg|_0^2 = \frac{8}{3}y + 2y^2 $$

$$ I = \int_0^1 \left(\frac{8}{3}y + 2y^2\right) dy = \left(\frac{4}{3}y^2 + \frac{2}{3}y^3\right)\Bigg|_0^1 = \frac{4}{3}+\frac{2}{3} = 2 $$

Evaluate $\displaystyle\iint_A (x^2y+xy^2)\,dA$ on $A=[0,2]\times[0,1]$.

2D Gauss Quadrature (Rectangular / Quadrilateral Domains)

Just as in 1D, map the physical rectangle $[a,b]\times[c,d]$ onto the standard square $[-1,1]\times[-1,1]$ in parent coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$:

$$x = \frac{a+b}{2} + \frac{b-a}{2}\xi, \qquad y = \frac{c+d}{2} + \frac{d-c}{2}\eta$$

$$dA = dx\,dy = \frac{(b-a)(d-c)}{4}\,d\xi\,d\eta$$

The 2D Gauss rule is simply the tensor product of two 1D rules — sum over every combination of the 1D points and weights in each direction:

$$ I = \iint_A f(x,y)\,dA \approx \frac{(b-a)(d-c)}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} w_i w_j\, f\big(x(\xi_i),\,y(\eta_j)\big) $$

An $n\times n$ tensor-product rule uses $n^2$ evaluation points and is exact for the same maximum polynomial degree, $2n-1$, in each coordinate direction, as the corresponding 1D rule.

Figure 7. The parent square $[-1,1]\times[-1,1]$ in $(\xi,\eta)$ coordinates with the $2\times2$ Gauss points marked — each is simply the 1D 2-point location $\pm1/\sqrt3$ applied independently in each direction.

$$x = 1+\xi, \qquad y = 0.5+0.5\eta, \qquad \frac{(b-a)(d-c)}{4} = \frac{(2)(1)}{4} = 0.5$$

A Note on Triangular Domains

The tensor-product construction above relies on the domain being a rectangle (or, after an isoparametric mapping, a quadrilateral). Triangular elements — such as the constant-strain triangle (CST) introduced in Lecture 9 — use a different natural coordinate system (area or barycentric coordinates $L_1,L_2,L_3$, with $L_1+L_2+L_3=1$) instead of a square parent domain, so their Gauss points and weights are tabulated separately rather than built from a 1D tensor product. The underlying idea is identical, however: replace the exact integral over the element with a weighted sum of function values at a handful of well-chosen interior points,

$$\iint_A f\,dA \approx A\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\,f(L_{1,i}, L_{2,i}, L_{3,i})$$

with weights $w_i$ that sum to $1$ (so that a constant function integrates to exactly its value times the area $A$).

15. Putting It All Together: Accuracy vs. Cost

The table below compares every method above on the same integral ($\int_0^4(-x^4+20x^2+6x+5)\,dx = 289.8667$), ranked by number of function evaluations — the real "cost" of a numerical integration scheme:

Method Function evaluations Computed $I$ Error
Gauss, $n=1$ 1 324.000 11.78%
Gauss, $n=2$ 2 295.556 1.96%
Gauss, $n=3$ 3 289.867 0.00%
Trapezoidal, $N=8$ 9 287.875 $-0.69\%$
Trapezoidal, $N=16$ 17 289.367 $-0.17\%$

Key takeaway (qualified): for smooth integrands and a fixed number of evaluations, Gauss quadrature is generally much more efficient than equally spaced Newton–Cotes rules like Trapezoidal — with only 3 evaluations, Gauss $n=3$ matches Trapezoidal's 17 evaluations here. In FEM, however, the integration rule must be chosen to match the element type, interpolation order, geometry mapping, material response, and possible reduced-integration strategy — "use more Gauss points" is not always the right answer, since under-integration is sometimes used deliberately (reduced integration) to control element behavior. Gauss quadrature is nonetheless the standard default for evaluating finite element stiffness and mass matrices, where each function evaluation may require an expensive constitutive-model calculation.

FEM preview

Every tool introduced in this lecture appears at once in the formula every element in this course will eventually use to compute its stiffness matrix:

$$ \mathbf{k}^{(e)} = \int_{\Omega^{(e)}} \mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{B}\,d\Omega \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} \mathbf{B}_g^T\mathbf{D}_g\mathbf{B}_g \,|J_g|\,w_g. $$

  • $\mathbf{B}$: derivatives of shape functions (Sections 1–6)
  • $\mathbf{D}$: material behavior
  • $|J|$: coordinate-mapping scale factor (Section 13)
  • $w_g$: Gauss weight (Section 13)

We won't derive this yet — just notice that differentiation, the mapping, and Gauss quadrature are already all present.

16. Why This Matters for FEM

This "math review" is not incidental to the course:

  • Differentiation recovers strain from displacement — the derivative relationship in Section 3 reappears constantly in FEM, wherever a strain, curvature, or stress field is computed from a displacement or potential field.
  • Partial differentiation (Section 6) extends this to 2D and 3D: strain components, shape-function gradients, and the isoparametric Jacobian (Lecture 10) are all built from partial derivatives with respect to more than one coordinate.
  • Integration builds the stiffness matrix and load vector: FEM stiffness matrices are integrals (over an element's volume or area) of products of shape-function derivatives.
  • Numerical integration, especially Gauss quadrature, is how those integrals are actually evaluated in code — element integrals are rarely available in closed form, so every finite element you assemble in this course will use a Gauss rule like the ones derived above.
  • 2D integration extends this directly to plane elements: quadrilateral element stiffness matrices use the tensor-product Gauss rule of Section 14, while triangular elements (CST, Lecture 9) use area-coordinate quadrature — the same weighted-sum idea, adapted to a triangular parent domain.
