Lecture 12 · Part 5: Two-Dimensional Elements

CEE 2333 — Introduction to the Finite Element Method

Lecture 12: Strains, Stresses and Instabilities of the Bilinear Element

Prof. Lev Khazanovich

Learning Objectives

  • Write the shape functions, the displacement interpolation $[N]$ and the strain–displacement matrix $[B]$ of the plane rectangular bilinear element in the centred coordinates $(x',y')$, and read off which strain fields it can represent
  • Explain the challenge with bending: straight element sides produce a spurious shear strain and an element that is too stiff in bending, and quantify the excess energy
  • Compute stresses $\{\sigma\}=[D][B]\{d^e\}$ and explain why they are most accurate at the Gauss points; extrapolate Gauss-point stresses to the corners and sides with $r=\xi\sqrt3$, $s=\eta\sqrt3$
  • Classify the eight nodal displacement modes of the element (3 rigid-body, 3 constant-strain, 2 bending) by their strain energy $U_e$
  • Define a zero-energy (hourglass) mode, show why a one-point Gauss rule creates two of them, and see how they combine into mesh mechanisms

Outline: (1) The rectangular bilinear element · (2) Strains and the matrix $[B]$ · (3) The challenge with bending · (4) Strain energy and the eight deformation modes · (5) Element and mesh instabilities (hourglass modes) · (6) Stress computation and extrapolation · (7) Algorithm and code · (8) Conclusion and key takeaways.

What is reused and what is new

  • Reused, not re-derived: the isoparametric four-node element, Gauss quadrature and the rank check of Lectures 9–11 (The Isoparametric Element, The Lagrange Element, Serendipity Elements); the plane-stress matrix $[D]$ of Lecture 6 (named $[D]$ so that $E$ stays Young’s modulus); $[K^e]=\int[B]^T[D][B]\,dV$ of Lecture 7.
  • New in this lecture: the rectangle written in centred coordinates $x',y'$, where every matrix is explicit; a hands-on look at what the element cannot do (bend without spurious shear), where to read stresses, and how an under-integrated element fails (zero-energy modes).

1. The Plane Rectangular Bilinear Element

Take the four-node element of Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) and make it a rectangle of width $2a$ and height $2b$. Place the origin of the local coordinates $(x',y')$ at its centre, so that

$$ x_1=x_4=-a,\quad x_2=x_3=a,\qquad y_1=y_2=-b,\quad y_3=y_4=b,\qquad -a\le x'\le a,\quad -b\le y'\le b . $$

Nodes are numbered counterclockwise from the lower left, as before. Since $x'=a\xi$ and $y'=b\eta$, the shape functions of Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) become

$$ \begin{aligned} N_1(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1-\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1-\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big), & N_2(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1+\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1-\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big),\\ N_3(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1+\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1+\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big), & N_4(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1-\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1+\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big). \end{aligned} $$

The Jacobian of the map is the constant matrix $[J]=\mathrm{diag}(a,b)$, so $\det[J]=ab$, $dA=ab\,d\xi\,d\eta$ and the area is $4ab$. Because the map is so simple, $[B]$ can be written down directly in $x',y'$ without the product $[B_1][B_2][B_3]$.

Approximation of displacements

With the eight nodal DOFs $\{d^e\}=\{u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2,u_3,v_3,u_4,v_4\}^T$, the displacement field is

$$ \begin{Bmatrix}u(x',y')\\v(x',y')\end{Bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}N_1&0&N_2&0&N_3&0&N_4&0\\0&N_1&0&N_2&0&N_3&0&N_4\end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix}u_1\\v_1\\u_2\\v_2\\u_3\\v_3\\u_4\\v_4\end{Bmatrix} =[N]^T\{d^e\},\qquad [N]=\begin{bmatrix}N_1&0\\0&N_1\\N_2&0\\0&N_2\\N_3&0\\0&N_3\\N_4&0\\0&N_4\end{bmatrix}. $$

(Here $[N]$ is the $8\times2$ array, so the interpolation is written with its transpose; it is the same $2\times8$ interpolation matrix as in Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element).) Each component is bilinear: $u=c_1+c_2x'+c_3y'+c_4x'y'$, and likewise for $v$.

2. Strains and the Matrix $[B]$

The strains follow by differentiating the displacement field, $\{\varepsilon\}=\{\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}\}^T=[B]\{d^e\}$, where row 1 holds the $x'$-derivatives of the shape functions (acting on $u_i$), row 2 the $y'$-derivatives (acting on $v_i$) and row 3 both. Differentiating the four shape functions gives

$$ [B]=\frac1{4ab}\begin{bmatrix} y'-b&0&b-y'&0&y'+b&0&-b-y'&0\\ 0&x'-a&0&-x'-a&0&x'+a&0&a-x'\\ x'-a&y'-b&-x'-a&b-y'&x'+a&y'+b&a-x'&-b-y' \end{bmatrix}. $$

(The third row is the engineering shear $\gamma_{xy}=\partial u/\partial y+\partial v/\partial x$, the quantity that pairs with $\tfrac{1-\nu}2$ in $[D]$.) For example, $N_1$ gives $\partial N_1/\partial x'=\tfrac{y'-b}{4ab}$ in row 1 and $\partial N_1/\partial y'=\tfrac{x'-a}{4ab}$ in row 3, column 1, exactly as above.

What strain fields can the element represent?

Every entry of $[B]$ is linear, but in a restricted way. Row 1 contains only $y'$, row 2 only $x'$, and row 3 both. Hence

$$ \varepsilon_x=\beta_1+\beta_2\,y',\qquad \varepsilon_y=\beta_3+\beta_4\,x',\qquad \gamma_{xy}=\beta_5+\beta_6\,x'+\beta_7\,y', $$

where the seven $\beta$’s are fixed linear combinations of the nodal DOFs. Carrying out the sums gives, for the terms that matter below,

$$ \beta_2=\frac{u_1-u_2+u_3-u_4}{4ab},\qquad \beta_4=\frac{v_1-v_2+v_3-v_4}{4ab},\qquad \beta_6=\beta_2,\qquad \beta_7=\beta_4 . $$

Three consequences.

  • The displacement field is bilinear, yet the strains vary only linearly, and only in a limited way: $\varepsilon_x$ varies with $y'$ but not with $x'$; $\varepsilon_y$ varies with $x'$ but not with $y'$.
  • So the element cannot make the axial strain $\varepsilon_x$ vary along its length. A beam whose bending moment changes along $x$ needs $\varepsilon_x\propto M(x)\,y$, a term $x'y'$ in the strain, which this element does not contain: it will struggle to model a beam with moment varying along the length.
  • The coefficient $\beta_2$ drives both $\varepsilon_x$ (through $y'$) and $\gamma_{xy}$ (through $x'$), and $\beta_4$ drives both $\varepsilon_y$ and $\gamma_{xy}$. You cannot create one without the other. This coupling is the root of the bending problem of the next section.
Check your understanding: which nodal pattern makes $\beta_2\neq0$ while all four $\beta_1,\beta_3,\beta_4,\beta_5$ stay zero?

$\beta_2=(u_1-u_2+u_3-u_4)/(4ab)$ is the alternating sum of the four $u$ values. Take $u_1=u_3=c$, $u_2=u_4=-c$ and all $v_i=0$, the field $u=c\,x'y'/(ab)$. Then $\beta_2=c/(ab)\neq0$, while $\beta_1=(-u_1+u_2+u_3-u_4)/(4a)=0$, $\beta_5=0$ (its $u$-part $a(-u_1-u_2+u_3+u_4)$ cancels) and $\beta_3=\beta_4=0$ because they contain only the $v_i$. The strains are $\varepsilon_x=\beta_2y'$ and $\gamma_{xy}=\beta_2x'$: zero at the centre of the element — this is mode 7 of Section 4; it has positive energy under the 2×2 rule and only becomes a zero-energy (hourglass) mode under the one-point rule.

3. The Challenge with Bending

The shape functions are linear along each edge, so the edges remain straight during deformation: they rotate and stretch but never curve. A rectangular block in pure bending needs its top and bottom edges to curve, with the vertical sides staying normal to them. The bilinear element can only tilt its sides and leave the top and bottom edges straight, so the right angle between neighbouring sides changes.

  • The change of angle is a shear strain. In real pure bending there is none, so this shear is spurious.
  • The element therefore resists even pure bending with both normal and shear stresses, stores extra energy, and is too stiff in bending.
  • It still converges properly with mesh refinement: the spurious shear is a discretization error that shrinks as the elements become small.

How much too stiff? A closed-form answer

Load one rectangle with the exact pure-bending displacement, $u=\kappa xy$, $v=-\tfrac\kappa2(x^2+\nu y^2)$. At the four nodes $v$ is the same constant ($-\tfrac\kappa2(a^2+\nu b^2)$), a pure translation, so the element sees only the bending mode $u_i=\kappa x_iy_i$, i.e. $u=\kappa\,x'y'$ with $\beta_2=\kappa$. Its strains are $\varepsilon_x=\kappa y'$, $\varepsilon_y=0$, $\gamma_{xy}=\kappa x'$. In plane stress ($t=1$):

$$ U_{\text{normal}}=\tfrac12\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\kappa^2\!\int y'^2dA=\frac{1}{1-\nu^2}\,U_{\text{exact}},\qquad U_{\text{shear}}=\tfrac12G\kappa^2\!\int x'^2dA=\frac{(a/b)^2}{2(1+\nu)}\,U_{\text{exact}}, $$

where the exact field has $\sigma_x=E\kappa y$, $\sigma_y=\tau_{xy}=0$ and $U_{\text{exact}}=\tfrac12E\kappa^2\int y'^2dA=\tfrac12E\kappa^2\cdot\tfrac{4ab^3}{3}$. Adding the two parts:

$$ \boxed{\;\frac{U_{\text{bilinear}}}{U_{\text{exact}}}=\frac1{1-\nu^2}+\frac{(a/b)^2}{2(1+\nu)}\;} $$

With $\nu=0.3$ this is $1.484$ for a square, $2.637$ for $a/b=2$ and $7.253$ for $a/b=4$, the numbers of Lecture 10 (The Lagrange Element). The first term, $1.099$, is a small Poisson-type error ($\varepsilon_y$ is forced to zero). The second term is the spurious shear, and it grows with the square of the aspect ratio: a slender element is much stiffer than the beam it represents. The 2×2 Gauss rule is exact here (the integrand is a polynomial of degree 2 in each variable), so this is not an integration error but a property of the shape functions.

4. Strain Energy and the Eight Deformation Modes

A nodal displacement vector $\{d\}$ stores the strain energy

$$ U_e=\tfrac12\{d\}^T[K]\{d\}=\tfrac12\{d\}^T\!\int_V[B]^T[D][B]\,dV\,\{d\}=\tfrac12\int_V\{\varepsilon\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon\}\,dV . $$

The element has $8$ DOFs and therefore eight independent modes. They fall into three groups:

1, 2rigid translation$v=c$ ; $u=c$$0$$0$ (correct)$0$
3rigid rotation$u=-\theta y',\ v=\theta x'$$0$$0$ (correct)$0$
4constant strain$u=\varepsilon x',\ v=\varepsilon y'$$\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y=\varepsilon$$>0$$>0$
5constant strain$u=-\varepsilon x',\ v=\varepsilon y'$$\varepsilon_x=-\varepsilon_y=-\varepsilon$$>0$$>0$
6constant strain$u=\gamma y'/2,\ v=\gamma x'/2$$\gamma_{xy}=\gamma$$>0$$>0$
7bending$u=c\,x'y'/(ab)$$\varepsilon_x\propto y'$, $\gamma_{xy}\propto x'$$>0$$0$ (zero-energy mode)
8bending$v=c\,x'y'/(ab)$$\varepsilon_y\propto x'$, $\gamma_{xy}\propto y'$$>0$$0$ (zero-energy mode)

Rigid-body motions must store no energy, and they do ($U_e=0$, correct). The three constant-strain modes must store energy ($U_e>0$). The two bending modes have zero strain at the centre ($\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y=\gamma_{xy}=0$ at $x'=y'=0$) but nonzero strain elsewhere. The 2×2 rule sees that strain; a rule that looks only at the centre does not. In other words, under the full 2×2 rule the null space is modes 1–3 (the kernel of the element); under the one-point rule it is modes 1–3 plus 7–8 (the kernel of the reduced element).

5. Element and Mesh Instabilities

An instability is known as a mechanism, a kinematic mode, an hourglass mode or a zero-energy mode. The name refers to a nodal displacement that is not a rigid-body motion but produces zero strain energy. If such a mode exists, the stiffness matrix is singular beyond the rigid-body modes, and the structure can deform without resisting.

The energy formula of Section 4 shows when this happens. With a quadrature rule of Gauss points $p$ and weights $W_p$,

$$ U_e=\tfrac12\sum_pW_p\,\det[J]\,t\;\{\varepsilon_p\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon_p\},\qquad \{\varepsilon_p\}=[B(\xi_p,\eta_p)]\{d\}. $$

Because $[D]$ is positive definite, $U_e=0$ only if every sampled strain vanishes. A mode that is not rigid but has zero strain at every Gauss point is invisible to the rule.

Example: bilinear element with one Gauss point ($n=1$)

The single point is the centre, $\xi=\eta=0$ with weight $2\times2=4$ and $\det[J]=ab$, so

$$ U_e=\tfrac12\{d\}^T\,\big(4ab\,t\big)\,[B(0,0)]^T[D][B(0,0)]\,\{d\}=\tfrac12\,(4abt)\,\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon(0,0)\} $$

($4abt$ is the volume of the element; apart from it the energy is the single product $\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}$). The element energy depends on the three strain numbers at the centre only. The 8 DOFs minus 3 rigid-body modes leave 5 deformation modes, but the centre supplies only three strains, so the matrix has rank 3: two modes escape. They are exactly the two bending modes 7 and 8 of Section 4, whose strains $\varepsilon_x=\beta_2y'$, $\gamma_{xy}=\beta_2x'$ (and the analogous pair for $v$) vanish at the centre:

$$ \varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y=\gamma_{xy}=0\ \text{at the centre}\quad\Rightarrow\quad U_e=0 . $$

These are the hourglass modes of the element. The full 2×2 rule samples four points at $x'=\pm a/\sqrt3$, $y'=\pm b/\sqrt3$, sees the nonzero strains there, and restores rank $8-3=5$. Section 7 confirms both ranks numerically.

Hourglass modes in a mesh

The danger is larger in a mesh than in one element. Adjacent elements share nodes, and an hourglass pattern of one element can continue consistently through its neighbours, so a whole patch deforms with every element in its own zero-energy mode. Take a $2\times2$ mesh of four elements. The checkerboard pattern $u_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}$ on the nodes $(i,j)$ is, in each element, a multiple of $x'y'$, so each element is in mode 7 with zero strain at its centre. For this free mesh the stiffness matrix with a one-point rule has rank 12 instead of $18-3=15$: three spurious mechanisms beyond the rigid-body modes. Those are the three patterns (b), (c), (d) of Fig. 6.12-2 of Cook et al.. Whether supports remove them depends on where the supports are, which is why the rank check of Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) should be run on the assembled problem too.

Interactive: hourglass patterns in a 2×2 mesh

Remedies. The mechanism is not a bug in the shape functions but in the rule that samples them. The simple remedy is to use the full 2×2 rule for the bilinear element. Reduced integration is attractive because it is cheaper and cures some over-stiffness, but it must then be combined with a device that suppresses the hourglass modes (“hourglass control”) or be checked, mesh by mesh, for singular systems. Lecture 13 (Shear Locking, Selective-Reduced Integration and Hourglass Control) builds that device and a second remedy for locking.

Check your understanding: the hourglass mode has zero energy, yet you computed in Section 3 that mode 7 stores energy with the 2×2 rule. Which statement is true?

Both. Mode 7 is an actual deformation of the element, and its true strain energy, $\tfrac12\int\{\varepsilon\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon\}dV$, is positive, with normal and shear parts as computed in Section 3. It is the one-point rule that reports zero, because it samples the strain only at the centre, where this mode has none. A zero-energy mode is therefore a flaw of the element as integrated, not of the displacement field itself.

6. Stress Computation

Stresses follow from Hooke’s law applied to the strains of Section 2:

$$ \{\sigma\}=[D]\{\varepsilon\}=[D][B]\{d^e\},\qquad \{\sigma\}=\{\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy}\}^T,\quad [D]=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\begin{bmatrix}1&\nu&0\\\nu&1&0\\0&0&\tfrac{1-\nu}2\end{bmatrix} $$

(plane stress). Since $[B]$ is not constant, the stress changes from point to point, and so the question is where to evaluate it. Three facts guide the choice:

  • Stresses and strains are more accurate at the Gauss points. The 2×2 Gauss points of the element (at $\xi,\eta=\pm1/\sqrt3$) are the best places to read $\{\sigma\}=[D][B]\{d^e\}$.
  • Gauss-point values can be interpolated or extrapolated to other points of the element, e.g. to the nodes where you want to plot or compare them. The results are usually more accurate than evaluating the stresses directly at those locations.
  • Stresses computed element by element are generally discontinuous across element boundaries, because only the displacement, not its derivative, is continuous.

Extrapolation from the Gauss points

Use the same bilinear interpolation, but with coordinates scaled so that the Gauss points sit at the corners of an auxiliary square. Define

$$ r=\sqrt3\,\xi,\qquad s=\sqrt3\,\eta\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \xi=\eta=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\ \text{gives}\ r=s=1 . $$

In $(r,s)$ the four Gauss points are the corners $(\mp1,\mp1)$ of the dashed square in Fig. 1 below, numbered $1(-,-),\ 2(+,-),\ 3(+,+),\ 4(-,+)$. The stress at any point $P$ is

$$ \sigma_P=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sigma_iN_i(r,s),\qquad N_i=\tfrac14(1\pm r)(1\pm s),\qquad \sigma\ \text{is}\ \sigma_x,\ \sigma_y\ \text{or}\ \tau_{xy}, $$

with $\sigma_i$ the stress at Gauss point $i$ and the signs matching the corner. The element corners are $A(-1,-1),\ B(1,-1),\ C(1,1),\ D(-1,1)$ and the mid-sides $E,F,G,H$ (bottom, right, top, left).

Example

Corner A. At $A$: $\xi=\eta=-1$, so $r=s=-\sqrt3$. The weights are

$$ \begin{aligned} N_1&=\tfrac14(1-r)(1-s)=\tfrac14(1+\sqrt3)^2=1+\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\approx1.866,\\ N_2&=\tfrac14(1+r)(1-s)=\tfrac14(1-\sqrt3)(1+\sqrt3)=-\tfrac12,\\ N_3&=\tfrac14(1+r)(1+s)=\tfrac14(1-\sqrt3)^2=1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\approx0.134,\\ N_4&=\tfrac14(1-r)(1+s)=-\tfrac12 . \end{aligned} $$

Their sum is $1.866-0.5+0.134-0.5=1$, as it must be. The stress at the corner is $\sigma_A\approx1.866\sigma_1-0.5\sigma_2+0.134\sigma_3-0.5\sigma_4$: the value at the nearest Gauss point is amplified and the neighbouring values are subtracted, so extrapolated corner values tend to be more extreme than the Gauss-point values. Any linear field is reproduced exactly, since $\sum N_i=1$ and $\sum N_i\,(r_i,s_i)=(r,s)$.

A beam in bending: stresses in practice

Return to the beam of Section 3, a cantilever of two bilinear elements loaded by a pure moment at the free end. The exact shear strain is zero everywhere. The finite-element $\gamma_{xy}$, and hence $\tau_{xy}=G\gamma_{xy}$, is the sawtooth of the plot: it equals $\kappa x'$ inside each element, jumps at the interface, and is nonzero at the 2×2 Gauss points $x'=\pm a/\sqrt3$. Two points to take away:

  • The sampling point matters. In this particular pure-bending case the spurious shear is zero at the element centre, so the one-point (centre) value is exact for $\tau_{xy}$, while the 2×2 Gauss points show $\pm\kappa a/\sqrt3$. For a general stress state the 2×2 points are the better default (as above), but you should always ask what a computed stress would be at a different sampling point before trusting it.
  • The jump between elements is a diagnostic: a large discontinuity of $\tau_{xy}$ or $\sigma_x$ across a shared edge tells you the mesh is too coarse for the stress gradient, exactly as in the bending example.

7. Algorithm and Code

Element matrix, stresses and checks for the rectangular bilinear element

  1. Input $a$, $b$, $E$, $\nu$, $t$ and the Gauss rule ($2\times2$ by default).
  2. Set $[K^e]=[0]_{8\times8}$. For each Gauss point $(\xi_p,\eta_p)$ with weight $W_p$: evaluate $[B(a\xi_p,\,b\eta_p)]$ from Section 2, and add $t\,W_p\,ab\,[B]^T[D][B]$.
  3. Rank check. $\mathrm{rank}[K^e]$ must be $8-3=5$. Rank 3 means the one-point rule: two hourglass modes.
  4. After solving, compute $\{\sigma_p\}=[D][B(\xi_p,\eta_p)]\{d^e\}$ at the four Gauss points (the most accurate values).
  5. Stress at any point $(\xi,\eta)$: set $r=\sqrt3\,\xi$, $s=\sqrt3\,\eta$ and evaluate $\sigma=\sum_i\sigma_iN_i(r,s)$ with $N_i=\tfrac14(1\pm r)(1\pm s)$, separately for $\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy}$.

8. Conclusion and Key Takeaways

  • The element. Rectangle $2a\times2b$, centred coordinates $x',y'$, bilinear shape functions, $\det[J]=ab$, $\{u,v\}^T=[N]^T\{d^e\}$ with 8 DOFs.
  • Strains. $[B]=\tfrac1{4ab}[\cdots]$, $3\times8$, with $\varepsilon_x=\beta_1+\beta_2y'$, $\varepsilon_y=\beta_3+\beta_4x'$, $\gamma_{xy}=\beta_5+\beta_6x'+\beta_7y'$ and $\beta_6=\beta_2$, $\beta_7=\beta_4$. $\varepsilon_x$ cannot vary along $x'$, so the element cannot follow a moment that varies along a beam.
  • Bending. Straight sides produce spurious shear; the element is too stiff in bending by the factor $\tfrac1{1-\nu^2}+\tfrac{(a/b)^2}{2(1+\nu)}$, growing with the square of the aspect ratio. It still converges as the mesh is refined.
  • Stresses. $\{\sigma\}=[D][B]\{d^e\}$ is most accurate at the 2×2 Gauss points; extrapolate with $r=\sqrt3\xi$, $s=\sqrt3\eta$ and $\sigma=\sum\sigma_iN_i$; at corner A the weights are $1.866,\,-0.5,\,0.134,\,-0.5$.
  • Modes. 3 rigid-body modes ($U_e=0$, correct), 3 constant-strain modes ($U_e>0$), 2 bending modes. A mode that is not rigid but has $U_e=0$ is a zero-energy (hourglass) mode.
  • One-point rule. $U_e=\tfrac12(4abt)\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}$: rank 3, so both bending modes become zero-energy modes; in a free $2\times2$ mesh this gives three mechanisms. The full 2×2 rule restores rank 5.
  • Looking ahead. Lecture 13 resolves the dilemma between the locking $2\times2$ rule and the singular one-point rule: selective-reduced integration and hourglass control. The choice of element and mesh follows later.
