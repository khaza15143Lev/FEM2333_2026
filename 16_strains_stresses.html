Part A. Equivalent Nodal Loads: The Element Load Vector

In Lecture 7 the energy principle produced the element equation $[K]\{d^e\}=\{f^e\}$. The stiffness side, $[K]$, was built for the four-node and nine-node elements in Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) and Lecture 10 (The Lagrange Element). Part A builds the right-hand side. Throughout, the nodal degrees of freedom are ordered $\{u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2,\dots\}$, so the load vector is ordered $\{f_{1x},f_{1y},f_{2x},f_{2y},\dots\}$, and the thickness $t$ is constant.

A1. Why load vectors?

The finite element equations are written node by node: one equilibrium equation per degree of freedom. Real loads rarely act at nodes. Soil pressure acts along an edge, gravity acts on every particle of the body, and a wheel load lands wherever the wheel happens to be. We need a rule that turns any such load into a set of forces at the nodes.

The work-equivalence principle

The rule is already contained in the energy formulation. The potential energy of the loads is

$$ W=\int_V \{u\}^T\{q\}\,dV+\int_{S_T}\{u\}^T\{p\}\,dS , $$

where $\{q\}$ is the body force per unit volume and $\{p\}$ is the surface traction. Insert the interpolation $\{u\}=[N]\{d^e\}$, where $[N]$ is the $2\times 2n$ interpolation matrix (Part B writes the same array as $[N]^T$, with $[N]$ stored as $2n\times2$), and pull the nodal displacements out of the integrals:

$$ W=\{d^e\}^T\underbrace{\left(\int_V [N]^T\{q\}\,dV+\int_{S_T}[N]^T\{p\}\,dS\right)}_{\{f^e\}} . $$

The bracket multiplies the nodal displacements exactly as a vector of nodal forces would. So $\{f^e\}$ is the set of nodal forces that does the same work as the real load for every possible nodal displacement. These are the consistent (or work-equivalent) nodal loads.

Key idea. Nodal forces are chosen so that the work they do, $\{d^e\}^T\{f^e\}$, equals the work of the real load for any displacement the element can represent. The shape functions decide how the load is split among the nodes.

Figure A1 shows the idea for the quadratic edge treated in Section A4: the real load and the three nodal forces do the same work for the displacement shape in which only the mid-node moves.

Why not split the load by tributary length or area?

For a four-node element with a uniform load, tributary splitting happens to give the right answer. For higher-order elements it does not. Section A4 shows that a uniform pressure on the quadratic edge of a nine-node element sends only one sixth of the total load to each corner and two thirds to the mid-side node. A naive one-third split would violate work equivalence. The shape functions must do the distributing.

A2. The master formulas

$$ x=\sum_i N_i(\xi,\eta)\,x_i,\qquad y=\sum_i N_i(\xi,\eta)\,y_i,\qquad [J]=\begin{bmatrix}\partial x/\partial\xi & \partial y/\partial\xi\\ \partial x/\partial\eta & \partial y/\partial\eta\end{bmatrix}. $$

A four-step recipe that works for every load.

  1. Identify the load: where does it act, and what are its components in global $x,y$?
  2. Express the location in natural coordinates (a point, an edge with $\xi$ or $\eta=\pm1$, or the whole element).
  3. Evaluate $[N]^T\{\text{load}\}$, including the length or area factor $dL$ or $\det[J]$, and integrate.
  4. Check that the nodal forces add up to the resultant of the real load.

A3. Concentrated forces

Suppose a force $\{T\}=\{T_x,T_y\}^T$ acts at an interior point $A$ with natural coordinates $(\xi_A,\eta_A)$. The displacement of $A$ follows from interpolation:

$$ u_A=\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i(\xi_A,\eta_A)\,u_i,\qquad v_A=\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i(\xi_A,\eta_A)\,v_i , $$

and the work of the real force is $W=T_xu_A+T_yv_A$. Choose the nodal forces

$$ F_{ix}=T_x\,N_i(\xi_A,\eta_A),\qquad F_{iy}=T_y\,N_i(\xi_A,\eta_A),\qquad i=1,\dots,9 . $$

Their work is

$$ W^E=\sum_i\big(F_{ix}u_i+F_{iy}v_i\big)=T_x\sum_i N_iu_i+T_y\sum_i N_iv_i=T_xu_A+T_yv_A=W $$

for any $u_i,v_i$. That is the work-equivalence requirement, and it is the same statement as $\{f^e\}=[N(\xi_A,\eta_A)]^T\{T\}$. (Formally this is the surface or body integral with a Dirac delta function for the load, which picks out the value of $[N]$ at the load point.)

Related case: a force applied on the edge of a four-node element

Take a rectangle of width $2a$ and height $2b$, centred at the origin, with nodes 1 to 4 counter-clockwise from the lower left. On the top edge $y'=b$ the shape functions of nodes 1 and 2 vanish and

$$ N_3(x',b)=\frac{(a+x')(b+b)}{4ab}=\frac{a+x'}{2a},\qquad N_4(x',b)=\frac{a-x'}{2a}. $$

A downward line force of total magnitude $F$ at $x'=x_0$ therefore gives

$$ f_{3y}=-F\,\frac{x_0+a}{2a},\qquad f_{4y}=-F\,\frac{a-x_0}{2a}. $$

This is the lever rule from statics: the nearer node takes the larger share and the shares add to $F$. (If the load is written as a traction $P_0\,\delta(x'-x_0)$ per unit thickness, then $F=tP_0$.) For a linear edge the shape-function rule and the lever rule coincide; for a quadratic edge they do not.

A4. Surface tractions

For a traction $\{p\}$ acting along an element edge,

$$ \{f^e\}=t\int_L[N]^T\{p\}\,dL . $$

Parameterize the edge by a natural coordinate $s$. Along a small step $\Delta s$ the edge advances by $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, so $\Delta L=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}$. Dividing and multiplying by $\Delta s$ and taking the limit,

$$ dL=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}\right)^{2}}\;ds . $$

On the loaded edge one natural coordinate is fixed at $\pm1$, and every shape function whose node is not on that edge vanishes identically. Only the two (four-node) or three (nine-node) edge nodes receive load.

A4.1 Four-node element

For a uniform traction on a straight edge of length $L$ the shape functions along the edge are linear, $\tfrac12(1\mp s)$, and $dL=\tfrac L2\,ds$. Each integral gives $\int_{-1}^{1}\tfrac12(1\mp s)\,\tfrac L2\,ds=\tfrac L2$, so for the two nodes $a,b$ at the ends of the edge

$$ \begin{Bmatrix}f_{a,x}\\f_{a,y}\\f_{b,x}\\f_{b,y}\end{Bmatrix} =\frac{tL}{2}\begin{Bmatrix}p_x\\p_y\\p_x\\p_y\end{Bmatrix}. $$

Each node gets half of the total $tLp$.

Example A2: traction on an inclined edge

A quadrilateral has nodes $(1,1),(5,1),(7,4),(1,3)$. Edge 2–3 runs from $(5,1)$ to $(7,4)$ and carries a uniform traction $\{p\}=\{p,0\}^T$ (constant, in the global $x$-direction). Its length is

$$ L_{23}=\sqrt{(7-5)^2+(4-1)^2}=\sqrt{13}. $$

Hence $f_{2x}=f_{3x}=\dfrac{t\sqrt{13}}{2}\,p$ and all other entries vanish.

If the traction is given as a normal pressure $p_n$ and a shear $p_s$, convert to global components first. With the unit tangent $\mathbf s$ (from node 2 to node 3) and the outward normal $\mathbf n$, $\{p\}=p_s\,\mathbf s-p_n\,\mathbf n$ when $p_n>0$ means compression. The integral needs $\{p\}$ in global axes because $[N]$ multiplies global $u,v$.

A4.2 A partial uniform load on a rectangular four-node edge

Let the top edge carry a downward traction $p_0$ only for $-a\le x'\le x_0$. Then

$$ f_{3y}=-t\int_{-a}^{x_0}p_0\,\frac{a+x'}{2a}\,dx'=-\frac{t\,p_0}{4a}(x_0+a)^2, \qquad f_{4y}=-t\int_{-a}^{x_0}p_0\,\frac{a-x'}{2a}\,dx'=-\frac{t\,p_0}{4a}(x_0+a)(3a-x_0). $$

Check: the two add to $-t\,p_0\,(x_0+a)$, the resultant of the load. For the full edge ($x_0=a$) each node carries $-t\,p_0\,a$, the half-and-half split of A4.1.

A4.3 Nine-node element: uniform load on a straight edge

On a straight edge with the mid-node at the centre, $dL=\tfrac L2\,ds$, and the three edge shape functions are $L_{0,2},L_{1,2},L_{2,2}$ with

$$ \int_{-1}^{1}L_{0,2}\,ds=\frac13,\qquad \int_{-1}^{1}L_{1,2}\,ds=\frac43,\qquad \int_{-1}^{1}L_{2,2}\,ds=\frac13 . $$

For a uniform traction $p$ the nodal loads are therefore

$$ f_{\text{corner}}=\frac{t\,p\,L}{6},\qquad f_{\text{mid}}=\frac{2\,t\,p\,L}{3},\qquad f_{\text{corner}}=\frac{t\,p\,L}{6}. $$

Remember. On a quadratic edge, uniform load splits $\tfrac16,\ \tfrac46,\ \tfrac16$ of the total, not $\tfrac13$ each. These are the Simpson’s-rule weights.

Example A3: uniform pressure on the top of the $10\times6$ element

The top edge is $\eta=1$ with $-1\le\xi\le1$. Here $\partial x/\partial\xi=5$ and $\partial y/\partial\xi=0$, so $dL=5\,d\xi$. Only nodes 4, 7 and 3 are active. A downward pressure of 10 ksi with $t=1$ gives, for the middle node,

$$ f_{7y}=-t\int_{-1}^{1}N_7(\xi,1)\,(10)(5)\,d\xi=-50\int_{-1}^{1}(1-\xi^2)\,d\xi=-\frac{200}{3}\approx-66.67 , $$

and for the corners $f_{3y}=f_{4y}=-50\cdot\tfrac13=-\tfrac{50}{3}\approx-16.67$. The total is $-100=-p\,L\,t$, as it must be.

The integral runs over the whole edge, $\xi\in[-1,1]$, not $[0,1]$. The values $50/3,\ 200/3,\ 50/3$ are often quoted as positive magnitudes; for the physical downward direction they enter $\{f^e\}$ with a minus sign.

A4.4 Nine-node element: a curved edge

Now move the middle top node down to $(10,4)$ so that the top edge becomes a parabola. The geometry map changes to

$$ x=10+5\xi,\qquad y=2+2\eta+\xi^2+\eta\xi^2 . $$

On $\eta=1$, $y=4+2\xi^2$, so $\partial y/\partial\xi=4\xi$ and the length element is no longer constant:

$$ dL=\sqrt{5^2+16\xi^2}\;d\xi . $$

Example A4: the same 10 ksi load on the curved edge

Take a vertical traction of 10 ksi per unit length of the curved edge, with $t=1$:

$$ |f_{3y}|=|f_{4y}|=\int_{-1}^{1}\tfrac12\xi(1+\xi)\,(10)\sqrt{25+16\xi^2}\,d\xi\approx 19.57, \qquad |f_{7y}|=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-\xi^2)(10)\sqrt{25+16\xi^2}\,d\xi\approx 70.68 . $$

The total is $2(19.57)+70.68=109.82$, which equals $10$ times the arc length of the edge ($\approx10.98$). The split is now $0.178,\ 0.644,\ 0.178$: close to, but not exactly, $\tfrac16,\tfrac46,\tfrac16$, because the curved edge is longer near its ends than the straight-edge rule assumes.

What does “10 ksi” mean on a curved edge? The calculation above treats 10 as a vertical traction per unit arc length. A true normal pressure would point along the local normal and change direction along the edge, so $\{p\}$ would depend on $\xi$ and have an $x$-component too. If the load were 10 per unit horizontal projection (a surcharge), you would use $dx=5\,d\xi$ in place of $dL$ and recover the straight-edge result $16.67,\ 66.67,\ 16.67$. State the load definition before integrating.

A5. Body forces

For a body force per unit volume $\{q\}$ (for gravity, $\{q\}=\{0,-\rho g\}^T$),

$$ \{f^e\}=t\iint[N]^T\{q\}\,dx\,dy=t\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\int_{-1}^{1}[N]^T\{q\}\,\det[J]\,d\xi\,d\eta . $$

A5.1 Rectangular four-node element

For the rectangle of A3 (width $2a$, height $2b$) and $\{q\}=\{0,-q_0\}^T$, $N_3=\dfrac{(a+x')(b+y')}{4ab}$ and

$$ f_{3y}=-q_0\,t\int_{-a}^{a}\!\int_{-b}^{b}\frac{(a+x')(b+y')}{4ab}\,dy'\,dx'=-q_0\,t\,\frac{(2a^2)(2b^2)}{4ab}=-q_0\,t\,a\,b . $$

The same holds at every node: each carries $q_0tab$, a quarter of the total weight $q_0t(4ab)$.

A5.3 Nine-node rectangle

With constant $\det[J]$ (rectangular element) only $\iint N_i\,d\xi\,d\eta$ is needed. It factors into one-dimensional integrals of $L_{0,2},L_{1,2},L_{2,2}$, which are $\tfrac13,\tfrac43,\tfrac13$:

$$ \iint N_i\,d\xi\,d\eta= \begin{cases}\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac19 &\text{corner nodes }1\text{–}4\\[1mm] \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac43=\tfrac49 &\text{mid-side nodes }5\text{–}8\\[1mm] \tfrac43\cdot\tfrac43=\tfrac{16}{9} &\text{centre node }9\end{cases} $$

The total is $4\cdot\tfrac19+4\cdot\tfrac49+\tfrac{16}9=4$, the area of the parent square. As fractions of the total load, the nodes carry $\tfrac1{36}$ (corners), $\tfrac4{36}$ (mid-sides) and $\tfrac{16}{36}$ (centre).

Example A6: gravity on the $10\times6$ element

With $\det[J]=15$, $t=1$ and $\{q\}=\{0,-q\}^T$, $f_{iy}=-15\,q\times(\tfrac19,\tfrac49,\tfrac{16}9)$: $-1.667q$ at each corner, $-6.667q$ at each mid-side node and $-26.67q$ at the centre. The sum is $-60q$, which is $q$ times the area of 60.

For contrast, the eight-node serendipity element of Lecture 11 gives negative corner loads for a uniform body force ($-\tfrac1{12}$ of the total at each corner and $\tfrac13$ at each mid-side node). The pattern of the load vector depends on the element family, which is why it must come from the element’s own shape functions.

A6. Numerical integration of load vectors

Closed-form integration worked above because the elements were simple. In a program every load vector is evaluated by Gauss quadrature, the same tool used for $[K]$:

$$ \{f_{\text{body}}\}\approx t\sum_i\sum_j W_iW_j\,[N(\xi_i,\eta_j)]^T\{q\}\det[J(\xi_i,\eta_j)],\qquad \{f_{\text{surface}}\}\approx t\sum_i W_i\,[N(s_i)]^T\{p\}\,\frac{dL}{ds}\Big|_{s_i}. $$

A short Python implementation

The two functions below reproduce Examples A3 to A5. They use only NumPy.

import numpy as np

def N4(xi, eta):
    return 0.25*np.array([(1-xi)*(1-eta), (1+xi)*(1-eta),
                          (1+xi)*(1+eta), (1-xi)*(1+eta)])

def dN4(xi, eta):                      # rows: nodes, cols: (d/dxi, d/deta)
    return 0.25*np.array([[-(1-eta), -(1-xi)], [ (1-eta), -(1+xi)],
                          [ (1+eta),  (1+xi)], [-(1+eta),  (1-xi)]])

def body_force_Q4(X, q, t=1.0, n=2):
    """Consistent nodal loads for a constant body force q = (qx, qy)."""
    pts, wts = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(n)
    f = np.zeros((4, 2))
    for xi, wi in zip(pts, wts):
        for eta, wj in zip(pts, wts):
            detJ = np.linalg.det(dN4(xi, eta).T @ X)
            f += t*wi*wj*detJ*np.outer(N4(xi, eta), q)
    return f.ravel()                   # (f1x, f1y, f2x, f2y, ...)

def edge_traction_3node(Xe, p, t=1.0, n=3):
    """Loads on a quadratic edge. Xe = coords of (corner, mid, corner)."""
    pts, wts = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(n)
    f = np.zeros((3, 2))
    for s, w in zip(pts, wts):
        Ns  = np.array([-0.5*s*(1-s), 1-s*s, 0.5*s*(1+s)])
        dNs = np.array([s-0.5, -2*s, s+0.5])
        dL  = np.hypot(*(dNs @ Xe))    # sqrt((dx/ds)^2 + (dy/ds)^2)
        f  += t*w*dL*np.outer(Ns, p)
    return f

A7. Checks and pitfalls

Always check the resultant

Because the shape functions sum to one, the nodal forces must sum to the resultant of the real load:

$$ \sum_i f_{ix}=\text{resultant in }x,\qquad \sum_i f_{iy}=\text{resultant in }y . $$

For a body force this is $q\,t\,A$; for a uniform edge traction it is $p\,t\,L$; for a point force it is the force itself. The check takes seconds and catches most mistakes (wrong $dL$, forgotten $\det[J]$, wrong limits).

Common mistakes

  • Using $d\xi$ in place of $dL$ or $dx\,dy$. The integral is over the physical edge or area, so the length or area scale factor must appear.
  • Integrating from 0 to 1. The natural coordinates run from $-1$ to $1$.
  • Splitting load equally among the edge nodes of a quadratic element. The correct split for a uniform load on a straight edge is $\tfrac16,\tfrac46,\tfrac16$.
  • Putting forces on nodes that are not on the loaded edge. Their shape functions are identically zero along the edge.
  • Mixed-up signs and axes. Give the traction in global axes; a downward load is negative $y$.
  • Wrong DOF ordering. Match the order of $\{f^e\}$ to $\{d^e\}$: $f_{1x},f_{1y},f_{2x},\ldots$
  • Mismatched node numbering. The order assumed by the shape functions (corners counter-clockwise from the lower left, then mid-sides 5–8, then centre 9) must match the connectivity.

What happens next

Each element’s $\{f^e\}$ is added into the global load vector $\{F\}$ at the rows of its global degrees of freedom, together with any loads applied directly at nodes. After the displacement boundary conditions are applied, the system $[K]\{d\}=\{F\}$ is solved. In the $10\times6$ example, the top-edge loads $16.67,\ 66.67,\ 16.67$ end up on the $v$-degrees of freedom of nodes 3, 7 and 4 (global rows 6, 14 and 8), and every other entry of $\{F\}$ is zero.

Part B. Strains, Stresses and Instabilities of the Bilinear Element

With both sides of $[K]\{d^e\}=\{f^e\}$ now available, Part B returns to the left-hand side and examines the simplest quadrilateral, the plane rectangular bilinear element: what its strain field can and cannot represent, where its stresses are most accurate, and how an under-integrated version fails.

1. The Plane Rectangular Bilinear Element

Take the four-node element of Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) and make it a rectangle of width $2a$ and height $2b$. Place the origin of the local coordinates $(x',y')$ at its centre, so that

$$ x_1=x_4=-a,\quad x_2=x_3=a,\qquad y_1=y_2=-b,\quad y_3=y_4=b,\qquad -a\le x'\le a,\quad -b\le y'\le b . $$

Nodes are numbered counterclockwise from the lower left, as before. Since $x'=a\xi$ and $y'=b\eta$, the shape functions of Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) become

$$ \begin{aligned} N_1(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1-\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1-\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big), & N_2(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1+\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1-\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big),\\ N_3(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1+\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1+\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big), & N_4(x',y')&=\tfrac14\Big(1-\tfrac{x'}{a}\Big)\Big(1+\tfrac{y'}{b}\Big). \end{aligned} $$

The Jacobian of the map is the constant matrix $[J]=\mathrm{diag}(a,b)$, so $\det[J]=ab$, $dA=ab\,d\xi\,d\eta$ and the area is $4ab$. Because the map is so simple, $[B]$ can be written down directly in $x',y'$ without the product $[B_1][B_2][B_3]$.

Approximation of displacements

With the eight nodal DOFs $\{d^e\}=\{u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2,u_3,v_3,u_4,v_4\}^T$, the displacement field is

$$ \begin{Bmatrix}u(x',y')\\v(x',y')\end{Bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}N_1&0&N_2&0&N_3&0&N_4&0\\0&N_1&0&N_2&0&N_3&0&N_4\end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix}u_1\\v_1\\u_2\\v_2\\u_3\\v_3\\u_4\\v_4\end{Bmatrix} =[N]^T\{d^e\},\qquad [N]=\begin{bmatrix}N_1&0\\0&N_1\\N_2&0\\0&N_2\\N_3&0\\0&N_3\\N_4&0\\0&N_4\end{bmatrix}. $$

(Here $[N]$ is the $8\times2$ array, so the interpolation is written with its transpose; it is the same $2\times8$ interpolation matrix as in Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element).) Each component is bilinear: $u=c_1+c_2x'+c_3y'+c_4x'y'$, and likewise for $v$.

2. Strains and the Matrix $[B]$

The strains follow by differentiating the displacement field, $\{\varepsilon\}=\{\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}\}^T=[B]\{d^e\}$, where row 1 holds the $x'$-derivatives of the shape functions (acting on $u_i$), row 2 the $y'$-derivatives (acting on $v_i$) and row 3 both. Differentiating the four shape functions gives

$$ [B]=\frac1{4ab}\begin{bmatrix} y'-b&0&b-y'&0&y'+b&0&-b-y'&0\\ 0&x'-a&0&-x'-a&0&x'+a&0&a-x'\\ x'-a&y'-b&-x'-a&b-y'&x'+a&y'+b&a-x'&-b-y' \end{bmatrix}. $$

(The third row is the engineering shear $\gamma_{xy}=\partial u/\partial y+\partial v/\partial x$, the quantity that pairs with $\tfrac{1-\nu}2$ in $[D]$.) For example, $N_1$ gives $\partial N_1/\partial x'=\tfrac{y'-b}{4ab}$ in row 1 and $\partial N_1/\partial y'=\tfrac{x'-a}{4ab}$ in row 3, column 1, exactly as above.

What strain fields can the element represent?

Every entry of $[B]$ is linear, but in a restricted way. Row 1 contains only $y'$, row 2 only $x'$, and row 3 both. Hence

$$ \varepsilon_x=\beta_1+\beta_2\,y',\qquad \varepsilon_y=\beta_3+\beta_4\,x',\qquad \gamma_{xy}=\beta_5+\beta_6\,x'+\beta_7\,y', $$

where the seven $\beta$’s are fixed linear combinations of the nodal DOFs. Carrying out the sums gives, for the terms that matter below,

$$ \beta_2=\frac{u_1-u_2+u_3-u_4}{4ab},\qquad \beta_4=\frac{v_1-v_2+v_3-v_4}{4ab},\qquad \beta_6=\beta_2,\qquad \beta_7=\beta_4 . $$

Three consequences.

  • The displacement field is bilinear, yet the strains vary only linearly, and only in a limited way: $\varepsilon_x$ varies with $y'$ but not with $x'$; $\varepsilon_y$ varies with $x'$ but not with $y'$.
  • So the element cannot make the axial strain $\varepsilon_x$ vary along its length. A beam whose bending moment changes along $x$ needs $\varepsilon_x\propto M(x)\,y$, a term $x'y'$ in the strain, which this element does not contain: it will struggle to model a beam with moment varying along the length.
  • The coefficient $\beta_2$ drives both $\varepsilon_x$ (through $y'$) and $\gamma_{xy}$ (through $x'$), and $\beta_4$ drives both $\varepsilon_y$ and $\gamma_{xy}$. You cannot create one without the other. This coupling is the root of the bending problem of the next section.
Check your understanding: which nodal pattern makes $\beta_2\neq0$ while all four $\beta_1,\beta_3,\beta_4,\beta_5$ stay zero?

$\beta_2=(u_1-u_2+u_3-u_4)/(4ab)$ is the alternating sum of the four $u$ values. Take $u_1=u_3=c$, $u_2=u_4=-c$ and all $v_i=0$, the field $u=c\,x'y'/(ab)$. Then $\beta_2=c/(ab)\neq0$, while $\beta_1=(-u_1+u_2+u_3-u_4)/(4a)=0$, $\beta_5=0$ (its $u$-part $a(-u_1-u_2+u_3+u_4)$ cancels) and $\beta_3=\beta_4=0$ because they contain only the $v_i$. The strains are $\varepsilon_x=\beta_2y'$ and $\gamma_{xy}=\beta_2x'$: zero at the centre of the element — this is mode 7 of Section 4; it has positive energy under the 2×2 rule and only becomes a zero-energy (hourglass) mode under the one-point rule.

3. The Challenge with Bending

The shape functions are linear along each edge, so the edges remain straight during deformation: they rotate and stretch but never curve. A rectangular block in pure bending needs its top and bottom edges to curve, with the vertical sides staying normal to them. The bilinear element can only tilt its sides and leave the top and bottom edges straight, so the right angle between neighbouring sides changes.

  • The change of angle is a shear strain. In real pure bending there is none, so this shear is spurious.
  • The element therefore resists even pure bending with both normal and shear stresses, stores extra energy, and is too stiff in bending.
  • It still converges properly with mesh refinement: the spurious shear is a discretization error that shrinks as the elements become small.

How much too stiff? A closed-form answer

Load one rectangle with the exact pure-bending displacement, $u=\kappa xy$, $v=-\tfrac\kappa2(x^2+\nu y^2)$. At the four nodes $v$ is the same constant ($-\tfrac\kappa2(a^2+\nu b^2)$), a pure translation, so the element sees only the bending mode $u_i=\kappa x_iy_i$, i.e. $u=\kappa\,x'y'$ with $\beta_2=\kappa$. Its strains are $\varepsilon_x=\kappa y'$, $\varepsilon_y=0$, $\gamma_{xy}=\kappa x'$. In plane stress ($t=1$):

$$ U_{\text{normal}}=\tfrac12\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\kappa^2\!\int y'^2dA=\frac{1}{1-\nu^2}\,U_{\text{exact}},\qquad U_{\text{shear}}=\tfrac12G\kappa^2\!\int x'^2dA=\frac{(a/b)^2}{2(1+\nu)}\,U_{\text{exact}}, $$

where the exact field has $\sigma_x=E\kappa y$, $\sigma_y=\tau_{xy}=0$ and $U_{\text{exact}}=\tfrac12E\kappa^2\int y'^2dA=\tfrac12E\kappa^2\cdot\tfrac{4ab^3}{3}$. Adding the two parts:

$$ \boxed{\;\frac{U_{\text{bilinear}}}{U_{\text{exact}}}=\frac1{1-\nu^2}+\frac{(a/b)^2}{2(1+\nu)}\;} $$

With $\nu=0.3$ this is $1.484$ for a square, $2.637$ for $a/b=2$ and $7.253$ for $a/b=4$, the numbers of Lecture 10 (The Lagrange Element). The first term, $1.099$, is a small Poisson-type error ($\varepsilon_y$ is forced to zero). The second term is the spurious shear, and it grows with the square of the aspect ratio: a slender element is much stiffer than the beam it represents. The 2×2 Gauss rule is exact here (the integrand is a polynomial of degree 2 in each variable), so this is not an integration error but a property of the shape functions.

5. Element and Mesh Instabilities

An instability is known as a mechanism, a kinematic mode, an hourglass mode or a zero-energy mode. The name refers to a nodal displacement that is not a rigid-body motion but produces zero strain energy. If such a mode exists, the stiffness matrix is singular beyond the rigid-body modes, and the structure can deform without resisting.

The energy formula of Section 4 shows when this happens. With a quadrature rule of Gauss points $p$ and weights $W_p$,

$$ U_e=\tfrac12\sum_pW_p\,\det[J]\,t\;\{\varepsilon_p\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon_p\},\qquad \{\varepsilon_p\}=[B(\xi_p,\eta_p)]\{d\}. $$

Because $[D]$ is positive definite, $U_e=0$ only if every sampled strain vanishes. A mode that is not rigid but has zero strain at every Gauss point is invisible to the rule.

Example: bilinear element with one Gauss point ($n=1$)

The single point is the centre, $\xi=\eta=0$ with weight $2\times2=4$ and $\det[J]=ab$, so

$$ U_e=\tfrac12\{d\}^T\,\big(4ab\,t\big)\,[B(0,0)]^T[D][B(0,0)]\,\{d\}=\tfrac12\,(4abt)\,\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon(0,0)\} $$

($4abt$ is the volume of the element; apart from it the energy is the single product $\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}$). The element energy depends on the three strain numbers at the centre only. The 8 DOFs minus 3 rigid-body modes leave 5 deformation modes, but the centre supplies only three strains, so the matrix has rank 3: two modes escape. They are exactly the two bending modes 7 and 8 of Section 4, whose strains $\varepsilon_x=\beta_2y'$, $\gamma_{xy}=\beta_2x'$ (and the analogous pair for $v$) vanish at the centre:

$$ \varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y=\gamma_{xy}=0\ \text{at the centre}\quad\Rightarrow\quad U_e=0 . $$

These are the hourglass modes of the element. The full 2×2 rule samples four points at $x'=\pm a/\sqrt3$, $y'=\pm b/\sqrt3$, sees the nonzero strains there, and restores rank $8-3=5$. Section 7 confirms both ranks numerically.

Hourglass modes in a mesh

The danger is larger in a mesh than in one element. Adjacent elements share nodes, and an hourglass pattern of one element can continue consistently through its neighbours, so a whole patch deforms with every element in its own zero-energy mode. Take a $2\times2$ mesh of four elements. The checkerboard pattern $u_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}$ on the nodes $(i,j)$ is, in each element, a multiple of $x'y'$, so each element is in mode 7 with zero strain at its centre. For this free mesh the stiffness matrix with a one-point rule has rank 12 instead of $18-3=15$: three spurious mechanisms beyond the rigid-body modes. Those are the three patterns (b), (c), (d) of Fig. 6.12-2 of Cook et al.. Whether supports remove them depends on where the supports are, which is why the rank check of Lecture 9 (The Isoparametric Element) should be run on the assembled problem too.

A zero-energy mode is therefore a flaw of the element as integrated, not of the displacement field itself.

6. Stress Computation

Stresses follow from Hooke’s law applied to the strains of Section 2:

$$ \{\sigma\}=[D]\{\varepsilon\}=[D][B]\{d^e\},\qquad \{\sigma\}=\{\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\tau_{xy}\}^T,\quad [D]=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}\begin{bmatrix}1&\nu&0\\\nu&1&0\\0&0&\tfrac{1-\nu}2\end{bmatrix} $$

(plane stress). Since $[B]$ is not constant, the stress changes from point to point, and so the question is where to evaluate it. Three facts guide the choice:

  • Stresses and strains are more accurate at the Gauss points. The 2×2 Gauss points of the element (at $\xi,\eta=\pm1/\sqrt3$) are the best places to read $\{\sigma\}=[D][B]\{d^e\}$.
  • Gauss-point values can be interpolated or extrapolated to other points of the element, e.g. to the nodes where you want to plot or compare them. The results are usually more accurate than evaluating the stresses directly at those locations.
  • Stresses computed element by element are generally discontinuous across element boundaries, because only the displacement, not its derivative, is continuous.

Extrapolation from the Gauss points

Use the same bilinear interpolation, but with coordinates scaled so that the Gauss points sit at the corners of an auxiliary square. Define

$$ r=\sqrt3\,\xi,\qquad s=\sqrt3\,\eta\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \xi=\eta=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\ \text{gives}\ r=s=1 . $$

In $(r,s)$ the four Gauss points are the corners $(\mp1,\mp1)$ of the dashed square in Fig. 1 below, numbered $1(-,-),\ 2(+,-),\ 3(+,+),\ 4(-,+)$. The stress at any point $P$ is

$$ \sigma_P=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sigma_iN_i(r,s),\qquad N_i=\tfrac14(1\pm r)(1\pm s),\qquad \sigma\ \text{is}\ \sigma_x,\ \sigma_y\ \text{or}\ \tau_{xy}, $$

with $\sigma_i$ the stress at Gauss point $i$ and the signs matching the corner. The element corners are $A(-1,-1),\ B(1,-1),\ C(1,1),\ D(-1,1)$ and the mid-sides $E,F,G,H$ (bottom, right, top, left).

Example

Corner A. At $A$: $\xi=\eta=-1$, so $r=s=-\sqrt3$. The weights are

$$ \begin{aligned} N_1&=\tfrac14(1-r)(1-s)=\tfrac14(1+\sqrt3)^2=1+\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\approx1.866,\\ N_2&=\tfrac14(1+r)(1-s)=\tfrac14(1-\sqrt3)(1+\sqrt3)=-\tfrac12,\\ N_3&=\tfrac14(1+r)(1+s)=\tfrac14(1-\sqrt3)^2=1-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\approx0.134,\\ N_4&=\tfrac14(1-r)(1+s)=-\tfrac12 . \end{aligned} $$

Their sum is $1.866-0.5+0.134-0.5=1$, as it must be. The stress at the corner is $\sigma_A\approx1.866\sigma_1-0.5\sigma_2+0.134\sigma_3-0.5\sigma_4$: the value at the nearest Gauss point is amplified and the neighbouring values are subtracted, so extrapolated corner values tend to be more extreme than the Gauss-point values. Any linear field is reproduced exactly, since $\sum N_i=1$ and $\sum N_i\,(r_i,s_i)=(r,s)$.

A beam in bending: stresses in practice

Return to the beam of Section 3, a cantilever of two bilinear elements loaded by a pure moment at the free end. The exact shear strain is zero everywhere. Two points to take away:

  • The sampling point matters. In this particular pure-bending case the spurious shear is zero at the element centre, so the one-point (centre) value is exact for $\tau_{xy}$, while the 2×2 Gauss points show $\pm\kappa a/\sqrt3$. For a general stress state the 2×2 points are the better default (as above), but you should always ask what a computed stress would be at a different sampling point before trusting it.
  • The jump between elements is a diagnostic: a large discontinuity of $\tau_{xy}$ or $\sigma_x$ across a shared edge tells you the mesh is too coarse for the stress gradient, exactly as in the bending example.

7. Algorithm and Code

Element matrix, stresses and checks for the rectangular bilinear element

  1. Input $a$, $b$, $E$, $\nu$, $t$ and the Gauss rule ($2\times2$ by default).
  2. Set $[K^e]=[0]_{8\times8}$. For each Gauss point $(\xi_p,\eta_p)$ with weight $W_p$: evaluate $[B(a\xi_p,\,b\eta_p)]$ from Section 2, and add $t\,W_p\,ab\,[B]^T[D][B]$.
  3. Rank check. $\mathrm{rank}[K^e]$ must be $8-3=5$. Rank 3 means the one-point rule: two hourglass modes.
  4. After solving, compute $\{\sigma_p\}=[D][B(\xi_p,\eta_p)]\{d^e\}$ at the four Gauss points (the most accurate values).

8. Conclusion and Key Takeaways

  • Load vectors (Part A). $\{f^e\}=\int[N]^T\{q\}\,dV+\int[N]^T\{p\}\,dS$ is the set of nodal forces that does the same work as the real load for every admissible nodal displacement. Use $[N]^T\{T\}$ at the load point for a concentrated force, $dL$ for tractions and $\det[J]$ for body forces; a uniform load on a straight quadratic edge splits $\tfrac16,\tfrac46,\tfrac16$. Always check that the nodal forces sum to the resultant.
  • The element. Rectangle $2a\times2b$, centred coordinates $x',y'$, bilinear shape functions, $\det[J]=ab$, $\{u,v\}^T=[N]^T\{d^e\}$ with 8 DOFs.
  • Strains. $[B]=\tfrac1{4ab}[\cdots]$, $3\times8$, with $\varepsilon_x=\beta_1+\beta_2y'$, $\varepsilon_y=\beta_3+\beta_4x'$, $\gamma_{xy}=\beta_5+\beta_6x'+\beta_7y'$ and $\beta_6=\beta_2$, $\beta_7=\beta_4$. $\varepsilon_x$ cannot vary along $x'$, so the element cannot follow a moment that varies along a beam.
  • Bending. Straight sides produce spurious shear; the element is too stiff in bending by the factor $\tfrac1{1-\nu^2}+\tfrac{(a/b)^2}{2(1+\nu)}$, growing with the square of the aspect ratio. It still converges as the mesh is refined.
  • Stresses. $\{\sigma\}=[D][B]\{d^e\}$ is most accurate at the 2×2 Gauss points; extrapolate with $r=\sqrt3\xi$, $s=\sqrt3\eta$ and $\sigma=\sum\sigma_iN_i$; at corner A the weights are $1.866,\,-0.5,\,0.134,\,-0.5$.
  • Modes. 3 rigid-body modes ($U_e=0$, correct), 3 constant-strain modes ($U_e>0$), 2 bending modes. A mode that is not rigid but has $U_e=0$ is a zero-energy (hourglass) mode.
  • One-point rule. $U_e=\tfrac12(4abt)\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}^T[D]\{\varepsilon(0,0)\}$: rank 3, so both bending modes become zero-energy modes; in a free $2\times2$ mesh this gives three mechanisms. The full 2×2 rule restores rank 5.
  • Looking ahead. Lecture 13 resolves the dilemma between the locking $2\times2$ rule and the singular one-point rule: selective-reduced integration and hourglass control. The choice of element and mesh follows later.
