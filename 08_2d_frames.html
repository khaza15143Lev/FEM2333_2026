Lecture 4

The Stiffness Method for 2D Frames

Extends the 2D truss stiffness method to rigid-jointed frame members that resist bending and shear as well as axial force: build the local \(6\times6\) frame-element stiffness matrix from an axial block and a Euler–Bernoulli bending block, transform it into global coordinates, assemble and solve the global system, and recover member end actions — with a fully worked L-frame example.

Subject: 2D frame (beam-column) elements Method: Direct stiffness with coordinate transformation (\([K]^{(e)}=[T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T]\)) Prerequisites: 2D truss stiffness method (Lecture 3), Euler–Bernoulli beam theory

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

  1. Contrast pin-jointed truss members with rigid-jointed frame members in terms of joint kinematics, internal force resultants, and section properties required.
  2. List the six local degrees of freedom of a two-node 2D frame element and state the Euler–Bernoulli kinematic assumptions used in its formulation.
  3. Assemble the local \(6\times6\) stiffness matrix \([K']^{(e)}\) by interleaving the axial \(EA/L\) block with the Euler–Bernoulli bending block derived from Hermite cubics (or from equilibrium and \(M=EI\kappa\)).
  4. Construct the \(6\times6\) transformation matrix \([T]\) (or its \(3\times3\) rotation blocks) and form the global-coordinate element matrix, \[ [K]^{(e)}=[T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T]. \]
  5. Assemble the global structural stiffness matrix, impose displacement boundary conditions, and solve for the unknown nodal translations and rotations of a small plane frame.
  6. Recover support reactions and the six local member end actions (axial force, shear force, and bending moment at each end) and interpret the load path through a rigid joint.
  7. Verify a completed analysis using global equilibrium (including moments), joint-by-joint equilibrium, an independent statics solution (when the frame is statically determinate), deformation reasonableness, matrix symmetry, and, optionally, an energy balance check.

Prerequisite review: This lecture builds directly on the stiffness method for 2D trusses. Everything about local vs. global coordinates, the 2×2 rotation block, and the assemble-partition-solve-recover procedure carries over unchanged — we are only adding one new degree of freedom per node and one new kind of internal force (bending moment) to a framework you have already built.

Part 1 — From Pin-Jointed Trusses to Rigid Frames

Everything in the 2D truss lecture rested on one modeling assumption: every member is a two-force member, pinned at both ends, so it can only push or pull along its own axis. That assumption is exactly right for a truss, and it is exactly what let us describe each member with a single local stiffness \(k=EA/L\) and a single local degree of freedom per node.

A frame relaxes that assumption in one specific way: the joints are rigid (welded or fully bolted) rather than pinned, so members meeting at a joint are forced to rotate together. A member framed into a rigid joint therefore does not just carry axial force — bending the joint bends the member, so the member must also resist shear and bending moment. Physically nothing about the member's material or cross-section has changed; what has changed is the connection, and that is enough to add an entirely new family of internal forces and an entirely new nodal degree of freedom.

PropertyTruss element (preceding lecture)Frame element (this lecture)

DOFs per element (2 nodes)46

Because the extra degree of freedom is a rotation about an axis perpendicular to the plane of the frame (the out-of-plane \(z\)-axis, in a 2D problem), it is unaffected by an in-plane rotation of the \(x\)–\(y\) axes: a member's local rotation \(\theta'\) and its global rotation \(\theta\) are literally the same number, \(\theta'=\theta\). Only the in-plane translations \(u,v\) need the \(c,s\) rotation from the 2D truss lecture. Keep that fact in mind — it is what keeps the frame transformation matrix almost as simple as the truss one.

Conceptual check. Can a pin-jointed truss member transmit a nodal moment? Answer: No — a two-force member can only push or pull along its own axis, so it transmits axial force only; no bending moment or shear can pass through a frictionless pin.

Part 2 — Building the 2D Frame Element Stiffness Matrix

1.1 Degrees of freedom of a 2D frame element

A 2D frame element still connects two nodes, \(i\) and \(j\), but each node now carries three degrees of freedom instead of two:

\[ \{u'\} = \{\,u_i',\ v_i',\ \theta_i',\ u_j',\ v_j',\ \theta_j'\,\}^T \]

in the member's own local axes, where \(u'\) is displacement along the member (axial, exactly as in the 2D truss lecture), \(v'\) is displacement transverse to the member (new), and \(\theta'\) is the end rotation (new). The element therefore has \(6\) local DOFs in total, up from \(4\) for a truss bar.

1.2 Local stiffness matrix: axial block + bending block

Element assumptions. The frame element derived below assumes: straight, prismatic, linearly elastic members; small displacements and rotations; plane-frame behavior (all loads and deformations in a single plane); rigid joints (members framing into a joint share a common nodal rotation); and Euler–Bernoulli bending, meaning plane cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the member axis, so shear deformation is neglected. This is why the local stiffness matrix below is built only from \(EA\) and \(EI\) and contains no shear-area term such as \(GA_s\). Relaxing these assumptions leads to Timoshenko beam elements (shear deformation), geometric nonlinearity (large displacements/rotations), or distributed-load formulations — topics beyond this lecture.

The two families of behavior — axial stretching and transverse bending — do not interact with each other in a straight, prismatic, linearly elastic member, so the local \(6\times6\) stiffness matrix is simply the two smaller matrices you already know, placed into the rows/columns of the DOFs they act on, with zeros everywhere else.

Axial block (acts on \(u_i',u_j'\)) — identical to the truss bar of the preceding lecture:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}k_a & -k_a\\-k_a & k_a\end{bmatrix}, \qquad k_a=\frac{EA}{L} \]

Bending block (acts on \(v_i',\theta_i',v_j',\theta_j'\)) — the classical Euler–Bernoulli beam element, derived from the beam differential equation \(EI\,v''''=0\) with cubic (Hermite) shape functions:

\[ \frac{EI}{L^3}\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 6L & -12 & 6L\\ 6L & 4L^2 & -6L & 2L^2\\ -12 & -6L & 12 & -6L\\ 6L & 2L^2 & -6L & 4L^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Deriving the bending block from a cubic displacement field

Unlike the axial block, the bending block is worth deriving rather than just quoting. The approach: assume a displacement shape, fix its free constants using nodal values, then read the stiffness directly off equilibrium — the elementary moment–curvature relation and shear–moment balance for a beam segment with no distributed load, pure statics with no reference to energy. (A strain-energy-based version of this same derivation, via the Rayleigh–Ritz idea, is covered in the upcoming Energy Methods lecture; the two approaches agree because they are the same physics viewed two ways.) Assume the transverse displacement along the member varies as a cubic polynomial in the local axial coordinate \(x\) (\(0\le x\le L\)):

\[ v(x) = a_1 + a_2x + a_3x^2 + a_4x^3 \]

Why a cubic, and why it's exact here: for a prismatic Euler–Bernoulli beam with no distributed load along its length, the governing equation is \(EI\,v''''(x)=w(x)=0\). Integrating \(v''''=0\) four times gives exactly a cubic. So this is not an approximation for the frame members in this lecture's worked example (no distributed load) — it is the exact Euler–Bernoulli solution. (It becomes an approximation, in the Rayleigh–Ritz sense, only once a distributed load or a non-prismatic section is introduced.)

Step 1 — express the four constants in terms of nodal values. The rotation is the slope, \(\theta(x)=v'(x)=a_2+2a_3x+3a_4x^2\). Evaluating \(v\) and \(v'\) at the two ends defines the four nodal DOFs:

\[ v_1=v(0)=a_1,\qquad \theta_1=v'(0)=a_2 \]

\[ v_2=v(L)=a_1+a_2L+a_3L^2+a_4L^3,\qquad \theta_2=v'(L)=a_2+2a_3L+3a_4L^2 \]

Solving this \(4\times4\) linear system for \(a_1,\ldots,a_4\) in terms of \(v_1,\theta_1,v_2,\theta_2\) gives \(a_1=v_1\), \(a_2=\theta_1\), and

\[ a_3=\frac{-3v_1-2L\theta_1+3v_2-L\theta_2}{L^2},\qquad a_4=\frac{2v_1+L\theta_1-2v_2+L\theta_2}{L^3} \]

Step 2 — collect into shape functions. Substituting back into \(v(x)\) and collecting the coefficient multiplying each nodal value gives

\[ v(x) = N_1(x)\,v_1 + N_2(x)\,\theta_1 + N_3(x)\,v_2 + N_4(x)\,\theta_2 \]

with the classical Hermite cubic shape functions:

\[ N_1=\frac{L^3-3Lx^2+2x^3}{L^3},\qquad N_2=x-\frac{2x^2}{L}+\frac{x^3}{L^2}, \qquad N_3=\frac{x^2(3L-2x)}{L^3},\qquad N_4=\frac{x^2(x-L)}{L^2} \]

These play exactly the role the linear shape functions played for the truss bar in the earlier spring-system and truss lectures: each \(N_i\) equals \(1\) (or has unit slope, for \(N_2,N_4\)) at its own DOF and vanishes (with vanishing slope) at every other nodal value — but here there are four shape functions instead of two, because matching both displacement and slope at each node forces a higher-degree polynomial.

Step 3 — moment and shear from curvature. For Euler–Bernoulli bending, two elementary relations from mechanics of materials connect the deflection to the internal bending moment and shear — pure statics, with no reference to energy:

\[ M(x) = EI\,v''(x), \qquad V(x) = \frac{dM}{dx} = EI\,v'''(x) \]

Because \(v(x)\) is cubic, \(v'''(x)\) is constant along the member — exactly what equilibrium of a member with no distributed load requires (\(dV/dx=-w=0\)). Substituting the shape functions of Step 2 and evaluating at the two ends gives

\[ M(0) = -\frac{4EI}{L}\theta_1-\frac{2EI}{L}\theta_2-\frac{6EI}{L^2}v_1+\frac{6EI}{L^2}v_2, \qquad M(L) = \frac{2EI}{L}\theta_1+\frac{4EI}{L}\theta_2+\frac{6EI}{L^2}v_1-\frac{6EI}{L^2}v_2 \]

\[ V(0)=V(L)=\frac{6EI}{L^2}\theta_1+\frac{6EI}{L^2}\theta_2+\frac{12EI}{L^3}v_1-\frac{12EI}{L^3}v_2 \]

Step 4 — internal resultants to nodal forces. Cutting the member free at each end, the outward normal of the free body points in \(-x\) at node 1 and \(+x\) at node 2, so the external nodal action that balances the internal stress resultant flips sign between the two ends — exactly the same bookkeeping that makes a truss bar's axial nodal force \(-N\) at node \(i\) and \(+N\) at node \(j\) in the 2D truss lecture. For bending this gives

\[ M_1=-M(0),\qquad M_2=M(L),\qquad V_1=V(0),\qquad V_2=-V(L) \]

Step 5 — read off the stiffness matrix. Substituting Step 3 into these four relations expresses each nodal force as a linear combination of \(v_1,\theta_1,v_2,\theta_2\) — and by the direct definition \(K_{ij}=\) force at DOF \(i\) per unit displacement at DOF \(j\), those coefficients are the stiffness matrix:

\[ V_1=\tfrac{12EI}{L^3}v_1+\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}\theta_1-\tfrac{12EI}{L^3}v_2+\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}\theta_2, \qquad M_1=\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}v_1+\tfrac{4EI}{L}\theta_1-\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}v_2+\tfrac{2EI}{L}\theta_2 \]

\[ V_2=-\tfrac{12EI}{L^3}v_1-\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}\theta_1+\tfrac{12EI}{L^3}v_2-\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}\theta_2, \qquad M_2=\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}v_1+\tfrac{2EI}{L}\theta_1-\tfrac{6EI}{L^2}v_2+\tfrac{4EI}{L}\theta_2 \]

— exactly the bending block quoted at the top of this section, recovered entirely from equilibrium and the moment–curvature relation, without ever forming a strain-energy expression. This equilibrium approach builds the stiffness matrix by asking what force is needed to hold a given displacement shape in balance — the same question a strain-energy (Rayleigh–Ritz) approach answers by asking how much work is stored in that shape. For a linear elastic member the two questions have the same answer, which is a useful fact to keep in mind before the Energy Methods lecture formalizes the connection between energy minimization and equilibrium.

Interleaving the axial and bending blocks in the local DOF order \(\{u'\}^{(e)}=\{u_i',v_i',\theta_i',u_j',v_j',\theta_j'\}^T\) gives the full local element stiffness matrix:

\[ [K']^{(e)} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0 & -\dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0\\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & 0 & -\dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2}\\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{4EI}{L} & 0 & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{2EI}{L}\\[6pt] -\dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0\\[6pt] 0 & -\dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & 0 & \dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2}\\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{2EI}{L} & 0 & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{4EI}{L} \end{bmatrix} \]

Two features are worth flagging before moving on, because both are common sources of assembly bugs:

  • The zeros in the "axial" rows/columns and the "bending" rows/columns are exact, not approximate — in an ideal Euler–Bernoulli member the two behaviors are genuinely uncoupled locally. They will not stay zero once we rotate into global coordinates (see §1.4).
  • \([K']^{(e)}\) is symmetric and singular before boundary conditions are applied, exactly like the truss case — translating and rotating the whole member as a rigid body takes zero energy, which is exactly what a stiffness matrix should say.

Conceptual check. Which local frame DOFs remain if axial deformation is neglected (i.e., the member is treated as inextensible)? Answer: \(v_i',\theta_i',v_j',\theta_j'\) — the bending DOFs; the axial DOFs \(u_i',u_j'\) drop out because there is no longer any freedom to stretch.

1.3 The transformation matrix for frames

Recall the 2×2 rotation block from the 2D truss lecture, built from the member's direction cosines \(c=\cos\phi,\ s=\sin\phi\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}u'\\v'\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}c&s\\-s&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix} \]

For a frame element we need this rotation to act on the \((u,v)\) pair at each node, while leaving each node's rotation untouched — because, as noted in Part 1, \(\theta'=\theta\). That gives a \(3\times3\) block per node:

\[ [R] = \begin{bmatrix}c&s&0\\-s&c&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

and the full \(6\times6\) transformation matrix is simply two copies of \([R]\) placed block-diagonally, one for node \(i\) and one for node \(j\):

\[ [T] = \begin{bmatrix}[R] & [0]\\ [0] & [R]\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \{u'\} = [T]\{u\},\quad \{f'\}=[T]\{f\} \]

This is exactly the same idea as the 2D truss lecture's \(4\times4\) \([T]\) — two copies of the same small rotation block, one per node — just with a \(3\times3\) block instead of a \(2\times2\) one, and a \(1\) sitting in the extra row/column so rotation passes through unchanged. \([T]\) is still orthogonal (\([T]^T=[T]^{-1}\)), for the same reason as before: it represents a pure rotation, which preserves lengths and angles.

Conceptual check. What changes for a horizontal element? Answer: \(c=1,\ s=0\), so \([R]\) becomes the identity and the local and global translational coordinates coincide — exactly the simplification used for member 2 in the worked example of §2.3.

1.4 The global element stiffness matrix

The transformation to global coordinates works exactly as it did for trusses — starting from the local force–displacement law \(\{f'\}=[K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\) and substituting \(\{u'\}=[T]\{u\}\), \(\{f\}=[T]^T\{f'\}\), gives

\[ \boxed{[K]^{(e)} = [T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T]} \]

with \([K']^{(e)}\) now the \(6\times6\) local element matrix of §1.2 and \([T]\) the \(6\times6\) matrix of §1.3, and \([K]^{(e)}\) the same element's stiffness expressed in global coordinates — not yet the assembled structural matrix \([K]\), which is built from every element's \([K]^{(e)}\) in §1.5. The arithmetic is the same triple product you carried out (or looked up) in the 2D truss lecture — there is just more of it, because axial and bending terms now mix together whenever the member is not perfectly horizontal or vertical. Carrying it out explicitly (see the Appendix for the full multiplication) gives, with the shorthand \(a=EA/L,\ b=12EI/L^3,\ d=6EI/L^2\):

\[ [K]^{(e)} = \begin{bmatrix} ac^2+bs^2 & (a-b)cs & -ds & -(ac^2+bs^2) & -(a-b)cs & -ds\\ (a-b)cs & as^2+bc^2 & dc & -(a-b)cs & -(as^2+bc^2) & dc\\ -ds & dc & \dfrac{4EI}{L} & ds & -dc & \dfrac{2EI}{L}\\ -(ac^2+bs^2) & -(a-b)cs & ds & ac^2+bs^2 & (a-b)cs & ds\\ -(a-b)cs & -(as^2+bc^2) & -dc & (a-b)cs & as^2+bc^2 & -dc\\ -ds & dc & \dfrac{2EI}{L} & ds & -dc & \dfrac{4EI}{L} \end{bmatrix} \]

Sanity check against the 2D truss lecture: if the bending terms are omitted (set \(I=0\), so \(b=d=0\)) and the rotational DOFs are removed, the remaining translational stiffness block reduces to the 2D truss-element matrix: \(k\begin{bmatrix}c^2&cs&-c^2&-cs\\ cs&s^2&-cs&-s^2\\ -c^2&-cs&c^2&cs\\ -cs&-s^2&cs&s^2\end{bmatrix}\). This demonstrates that the truss and frame formulations share the same axial component, and it is a useful algebraic self-check to run on the boxed matrix above any time you re-derive or re-implement it. It is only an algebraic limit, though — a physical frame element with \(I=0\) would have no bending stiffness at all and is not itself a valid frame model; it does not mean a frame element is "really" a truss element with the rotational DOFs simply along for the ride.

Just like the truss element, \([K]^{(e)}\) is symmetric, and it is singular before boundary conditions are applied — a free frame element can still translate and rotate as a rigid body without storing energy.

1.5 Assembly into the global system

Assembly proceeds exactly as in the 2D truss lecture's assembly section — add each entry of a member's global \(6\times6\) element matrix \([K]^{(e)}\) into the rows/columns of the structure-level matrix \([K]\) that match its global DOFs — with one bookkeeping change: because each node now owns three DOFs instead of two, node \(i\) (numbered from \(0\), following the same convention as the 2D truss lecture) occupies global DOF indices

\[ 3i,\quad 3i+1,\quad 3i+2 \qquad (u,\ v,\ \theta\ \text{respectively}) \]

instead of \(2i,\,2i+1\). A structure with \(n\) nodes therefore has a global stiffness matrix of size \(3n\times3n\), not \(2n\times2n\). This is the single biggest source of indexing mistakes when adapting truss code to frames — double every "2" that represented "DOFs per node" to a "3," and recheck every loop bound.

Step 4 — Develop the Element Stiffness Matrices

2.3 Transform and assemble

Member 1 is vertical (\(c=0,\ s=1\)), so its transformation swaps the roles of axial and transverse behavior: the column's global horizontal stiffness comes entirely from its local bending block, and its global vertical stiffness comes entirely from its local axial block. Member 2 is horizontal (\(c=1,\ s=0\)), so — as a special case of the boxed formula in §1.4 with \(c=1,s=0\) — its local and global matrices coincide exactly, just as an aligned bar's did in the 2D truss lecture.

Building each member's global \(6\times6\) matrix via \([K]^{(e)}=[T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T]\) and adding it into the rows/columns for its global DOFs gives a \(9\times9\) global system (\(3\) nodes × \(3\) DOFs each). Numbering DOFs \((u_1,v_1,\theta_1,u_2,v_2,\theta_2,u_3,v_3,\theta_3)\) as indices \(0\)–\(8\), and with the only load being \(F_x=10{,}000\) N at DOF 6 (\(u_3\)), the boundary conditions fix DOFs 0, 1, 2 (node 1: \(u_1=v_1=\theta_1=0\)), leaving a \(6\times6\) reduced system to solve for \((u_2,v_2,\theta_2,u_3,v_3,\theta_3)\) — the same partition-and-solve procedure as the 2D truss lecture, just with a \(6\times6\) reduced system instead of a \(3\times3\) one.

2.4 Solve for displacements

Before solving, it helps to see which element contributes to each block of the reduced \(6\times6\) system. With node 1 restrained, only node 2 and node 3 remain, in the order \((u_2,v_2,\theta_2,u_3,v_3,\theta_3)\). Member 1 (column, node 1→2) contributes only its node-\(j\) block, since its node-\(i\) row/column (node 1) was removed by the boundary condition; member 2 (beam, node 2→3) contributes all four of its blocks, since neither of its nodes is restrained. Writing \(\big([K]^{(m)}\big)_{ii},\big([K]^{(m)}\big)_{ij},\ldots\) for the node-\(i\)/node-\(j\) sub-blocks of member \(m\)'s global \(6\times6\) matrix \([K]^{(m)}\) (the same \(3\times3\) quadrants built in the Appendix), the reduced matrix has the block structure

\[ [K_{ff}] = \begin{bmatrix} \big([K]^{(1)}\big)_{jj}+\big([K]^{(2)}\big)_{ii} & \big([K]^{(2)}\big)_{ij}\\[4pt] \big([K]^{(2)}\big)_{ji} & \big([K]^{(2)}\big)_{jj} \end{bmatrix} \]

i.e., node 2's diagonal block sums contributions from both members that meet there (the defining feature of assembly at a shared node), while node 3's diagonal block comes from member 2 alone, since only member 2 touches node 3. This block pattern — diagonal blocks summed over every member meeting at that node, off-diagonal blocks contributed by whichever single member connects that pair of nodes — is exactly what §1.5's assembly rule produces, and it is worth checking against the actual code or hand assembly before trusting the solved numbers below.

Solving \([K_{ff}]\{u_f\}=\{F_f\}\) (numerically — the arithmetic is routine but tedious to carry by hand at \(6\times6\); this is precisely the kind of step FEM software exists to automate) gives:

Conceptual check. Before reading on: why is \(v_2\) exactly zero here, rather than just small? Answer below.

Why \(v_2\) is exactly zero, not just small: the only load is horizontal, so summing vertical forces on the whole structure forces the vertical reaction \(R_y\) at node 1 to be exactly zero. Member 1 (the column) is the only member that can carry a vertical force into node 1, and it can only do so through its axial force — so that axial force must itself be exactly zero. Since \(v_2\) is nothing more than the column's axial elongation (\(v_2 = NL/EA\) for this vertical member), zero axial force means zero elongation, not an elongation too small to notice. This is a useful general habit: before trusting a "looks like zero" entry in a results table, ask whether the structure's equilibrium actually forces it to be zero, or whether it is merely small.

A first physical read of these numbers is itself a useful check: the lateral load pushes node 3 sideways (\(u_3>0\), in the direction of \(P\)) and pulls it down as the corner rotates open (\(v_3<0\)) — both consistent with a cantilevered L-bracket pushed at its tip.

2.6 Member end forces: axial, shear, and moment

Sign convention. For each member, the local axis \(x'\) points from node \(i\) to node \(j\); positive \(N\) is tension; positive \(V\) and \(M\) follow the displayed local-element sign convention (the same one used in deriving the bending block in §1.2). Recall the local DOF order \(\{u'\}^{(e)}=\{u_i',v_i',\theta_i',u_j',v_j',\theta_j'\}^T\) from §1.1 — the local end-action vector \(\{q'\}=[K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\) (written \(\{f'\}\) in the code and tables below; the two symbols mean the same vector) follows that same DOF order and represents the node-on-element end actions: the forces and moments the nodes must apply to the element to produce the computed deformation \(\{u'\}\) — equivalently, the member's own resisting forces. By Newton's third law, the actions the element exerts back on its nodes are the negative of this vector, \(-\{q'\}\). The free-body diagrams below (e.g. Fig. 2) draw \(\{q'\}\) directly, as the forces holding the member's own free body in equilibrium; the joint-by-joint equilibrium check further on sums \(-\{q'\}\) from every member meeting at a joint, since that check asks what each member exerts on the joint, not what the joint exerts on the member. One convention — node-on-element for \(\{q'\}\) itself, element-on-node for its negative — is used consistently throughout the results below, and it matters again once distributed member loads are introduced (see "Looking Ahead" and the recovery equation given there), since the local nodal-load vector a distributed load contributes must be combined with \([K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\) using an explicit, unambiguous sign rather than treated as a same-sign "equivalent load" added on top.

Transforming each member's global end displacements back into local coordinates with \([T]\), then applying \(\{f'\}=[K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\), recovers the six local end actions \((N_i,V_i,M_i,N_j,V_j,M_j)\) for each member. This direct product is exact here because every load in this example is applied at a node — neither member carries a distributed or other element load; the more general case, where a member's own local nodal-load vector must also be subtracted, is developed in §3, “Looking Ahead.”

Skew the geometry, add a vertical load, or change the number of members meeting at a joint, and most of these zeros disappear.

The pattern here is worth pausing on, because it is a clean illustration of how a rigid frame joint redistributes load between members of different orientation: the lateral load applied at the free tip of the beam travels down the beam almost entirely as axial force (the beam is horizontal, and the load is horizontal), reaches the rigid corner essentially unchanged, and is then handed off to the column as shear — because what was "along the member" for the horizontal beam is "across the member" for the vertical column. The bending moment builds up linearly in the column, from \(0\) at the top (free to rotate together with the beam, no restraint there) to its maximum at the fixed base.

Part 4 — Checking the Results

The checks below are the same five checks from the "Checking the Results" section of the 2D truss lecture, extended to cover moments the way trusses never needed to.

Global equilibrium check

Sum forces and moments on the whole structure, loads and reactions together:

\[ \sum F_x = P + R_x = 10.0 - 10.0 = 0\ \checkmark \qquad \sum F_y = R_y = 0\ \checkmark \]

Taking moments about node 1 (the base), the applied load \(P\) acts at height \(3\) m above the base, so its moment about node 1 is \(P\times 3 = 30.0\) kN·m; the reaction moment \(M_1=30.0\) kN·m exactly balances it:

\[ \sum M_{(\text{node 1})} = M_1 - P\,(3\ \text{m}) = 30.0 - 30.0 = 0\ \checkmark \]

Joint-by-joint equilibrium check

At the free (unsupported) rigid joint, node 2, the forces each member exerts on the joint must balance in \(x\), \(y\), and moment — this last condition is new relative to trusses, and is exactly the statement that the joint is rigid: the moment the column delivers to the joint must equal the moment the beam takes away from it. Per the sign convention fixed in §2.6, the table there reports each member's \(\{q'\}\) (node-on-element) end actions, so the action each member exerts on the joint is \(-\{q'\}\) at that member's end; summing \(-\{q'\}\) from both members plus any external load at the joint (zero here) must vanish. Concretely, in moment: the column's top-end moment and the beam's left-end moment are both \(\approx0\), so their negatives balance trivially.

The \(x\)-direction balance takes more care, because the column and the beam do not share the same local \(x'\)-axis, so their local end actions must each be resolved into global \(x\) separately before they can be compared or summed. The column runs from node 1 to node 2 (vertically, \(c=0,\ s=1\)), so its local \(x'\)-axis points in global \(+y\) and its local \(+v'\)-axis — 90° from \(x'\) in the same sense fixed in §1.3 — points in global \(-x\), not \(+x\). From the table in §2.6, the column's top-end (node-on-element) shear is \(q'=-10.0\) kN along local \(+v'\); since local \(+v'\) is global \(-x\), that same vector is \(+10.0\) kN in global \(x\), so the column's element-on-node action at the joint, \(-q'\), is \(\mathbf{-10.0}\) kN in global \(x\). The beam runs from node 2 to node 3 (horizontally, \(c=1,\ s=0\)), so its local \(x'\)-axis already coincides with global \(x\) and no rotation is needed: the beam's left-end (node-on-element) axial force is \(q'=-10.0\) kN along local \(x'\), which is \(-10.0\) kN in global \(x\) directly, so the beam's element-on-node action, \(-q'\), is \(\mathbf{+10.0}\) kN in global \(x\). Summing the two element-on-node actions on the joint, \(-10.0+10.0=0\) kN — the column and the beam pull the joint in opposite global directions with equal magnitude, so they cancel exactly, consistent with no external load being applied at node 2 itself.

Independent hand solution

This particular frame is statically determinate: one fixed support supplies exactly three reactions (\(R_x, R_y, M_1\)), matching the three global equilibrium equations available for the structure as a whole, and the frame has no closed loops that would make it indeterminate. That means the reactions — and, by free-body cuts through each member, the axial force, shear, and moment at any cross-section — can be found from statics alone, with no stiffness matrix at all:

  • Base shear: cutting anywhere along the column and considering the free body above the cut, horizontal equilibrium requires the column to carry the full applied load as shear, \(V=P=10.0\) kN, at every column cross-section.
  • Base moment: taking moments about the cut, \(M(y) = P\,(3-y)\) for a cut at height \(y\) up the column — linear, zero at the top (\(y=3\)), and \(P\times3=30.0\) kN·m at the base (\(y=0\)).
  • Beam axial force: cutting through the beam and considering the free body from the cut to the free tip, the only horizontal force present is \(P\) itself, so the beam carries a constant axial force of \(10.0\) kN along its full length, with zero shear and zero moment (no transverse load is applied along the beam).

Every one of these hand-calculated values matches the stiffness-method output in the table of §2.6 exactly. This agreement is the strongest available confirmation that the assembly, boundary conditions, and recovery steps were all done correctly — precisely the same role the method-of-joints check played for the determinate truss in the 2D truss lecture.

Sign and physical reasonableness checks

  • The fixed node shows exactly zero displacement and zero rotation — any nonzero value there signals a boundary-condition bug.
  • The free tip (node 3) deflects in the direction of the applied load (\(u_3>0\), matching \(+P\)) and rotates in the sense that opens up the corner — both match intuition for a cantilevered bracket pushed sideways at its tip.
  • The bending moment is largest where physical intuition says it should be: at the fixed base, farthest (in load path) from the free end and carrying the full accumulated effect of the load acting through the column's height.

Stiffness matrix checks

  • The global \(9\times9\) stiffness matrix must be symmetric before boundary conditions are applied, for the same reason as the truss case: each element matrix \([K]^{(e)}=[T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T]\) is symmetric by construction, and superposition preserves symmetry.
  • A stable planar frame model must eliminate its three rigid-body modes: global translation in \(x\), global translation in \(y\), and rigid-body rotation. This generally requires three independent displacement restraints arranged so that no rigid-body mechanism remains — but, unlike the DOF count alone, the arrangement matters more than the count. A frame does not need a separately restrained nodal rotational DOF if the translational restraints are geometrically independent: a pin plus a roller, for instance, can stabilize a planar frame without directly restraining any nodal rotation. Conversely, three restraints that are not geometrically independent (e.g., three rollers all normal to the same direction) can still leave a rigid-body mechanism. If the reduced stiffness matrix is unexpectedly singular, check for exactly this kind of geometric dependence among the restraints, not just the restraint count.

Energy check (optional)

The external work must equal the total strain energy, now summing bending energy alongside axial energy in every member:

\[ \tfrac12\sum_i P_i\,\delta_i \;=\; \sum_{\text{members}}\left(\frac{N^2L}{2EA} \;+\; \int_0^L \frac{M(x)^2}{2EI}\,dx\right) \]

where the bending term integrates the (generally varying) moment along each member's length — for the column above, with \(M(y)=P(3-y)\) linear in \(y\), that integral can be evaluated in closed form; for a member with more complex loading, numerical integration (see the earlier lecture on differentiation and integration) is the natural tool.

Looking Ahead — Loads Beyond a Point Force at a Node

In this example, all external loading is applied directly at a node, so the global load vector \(\{F\}\) contains only a point force. Frame loading in general is richer than this: a member may carry a distributed transverse load (e.g., self-weight or wind pressure along its length), a distributed axial load, a thermal effect (uniform or gradient temperature change), a support settlement (a prescribed nonzero displacement rather than a prescribed zero), or an applied nodal moment. None of these fit the simple picture of "a force at a node."

For member loads such as a uniform distributed load, FEM does not treat it as a vaguely "statically equivalent" set of nodal forces — it defines a precise local nodal-load vector, built from the same shape functions \([N]\) used to build the stiffness matrix in §1.2:

\[ \{p'\}_e = \int_0^L [N]^T \{q\}\,dx \]

where \(\{q\}\) is the distributed load per unit length (its axial and transverse components, in local coordinates) acting on the member and \([N]\) is the same \(6\)-column matrix of local shape functions — the linear axial pair and the Hermite cubic bending set — evaluated along \(x\). This vector follows the same sign convention fixed in §2.6 for \(\{q'\}\): \(\{p'\}_e\) is node-on-element — it is built to enter the element's own local equilibrium alongside \([K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\), not as a separately-signed correction applied afterward.

Because the structural system is assembled in global coordinates, \(\{p'\}_e\) is rotated the same way any other local end-action vector is (§1.4): its global contribution is \([T]^T\{p'\}_e\), added into the global load vector alongside whatever is applied directly at nodes:

\[ \{F\} = \{F_{\mathrm{nodal}}\} + \sum_e [T]^T\{p'\}_e \]

Recovering member end actions afterward is correspondingly not just \([K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\). Once the global displacements are solved for and rotated back into local coordinates, the recovery equation — consistent with the node-on-element convention fixed in §2.6 — is

\[ \{q'\}_e = [K']_e\{u'\}_e - \{p'\}_e \]

i.e. the local nodal-load vector is subtracted from the raw stiffness product, and subtracted exactly once: because \(\{p'\}_e\) already carries the node-on-element sign built into its own definition above, adding it back in with the opposite sign, or adding an "equivalent load" a second time on top of a \(\{p'\}_e\) already folded into \(\{F\}\), double-counts the load and recovers an internal force with the wrong sign. This is the same idea sometimes described elsewhere as combining "equivalent nodal loads" going in with "fixed-end actions" coming back out — stated here directly in terms of \(\{p'\}_e\) so there is exactly one sign convention to track, applied consistently in both directions, rather than two conventions that have to be reconciled by hand. This subtraction is the central new bookkeeping step of the next frame-analysis lecture; nothing about the element stiffness matrix, transformation, or assembly developed here changes.

Summary: The 2D Frame Stiffness-Method "Recipe"

For every member in the frame:

  1. From nodal coordinates, compute the length \(L\) and direction cosines \(c=\cos\phi,\ s=\sin\phi\) — unchanged from trusses.
  2. Compute the member's axial stiffness \(k_a=EA/L\) and its bending terms \(12EI/L^3,\ 6EI/L^2,\ 4EI/L,\ 2EI/L\) — this is the one genuinely new ingredient, requiring the section's moment of inertia \(I\) in addition to its area \(A\).
  3. Build the member's local \(6\times6\) stiffness matrix \([K']^{(e)}\) as the axial block and bending block interleaved (§1.2), then form its global \(6\times6\) matrix \([K]^{(e)}=[T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T]\) using the \(6\times6\) block-diagonal \([T]\) of §1.3.
  4. Assemble: add each entry of \([K]^{(e)}\) into the rows/columns of the global system \([K]\) matching that member's global DOFs. With \(0\)-indexed nodes, node \(i\)'s three DOFs sit at global indices \(3i,\ 3i+1,\ 3i+2\) — the frame analogue of the truss's \(2i,\ 2i+1\) from the 2D truss lecture.

Then, once for the whole structure — identical in spirit to the structure-level assembly steps of the 2D truss lecture:

  1. Assemble the global load vector \(\{F\}\): loads applied directly at nodes (including applied moments) go in as \(\{F_{\mathrm{nodal}}\}\), and for any member carrying a distributed or other element load, its local nodal-load vector \(\{p'\}_e=\int_0^L[N]^T\{q\}\,dx\) (§3, “Looking Ahead”) contributes \([T]^T\{p'\}_e\), added into \(\{F\}\) exactly once per member — never added again later.
  2. Partition into known/unknown displacements and known/unknown forces (now three per restrained or free node); solve \([K_{11}]\{u_{unknown}\}=\{F_{known}\}-[K_{12}]\{u_{known}\}\).
  3. Back-substitute for the reactions (now including reaction moments at any rotationally restrained support) and check global equilibrium, including moment equilibrium, as a self-consistency check.
  4. For each member, transform its global end displacements to local coordinates, \(\{u'\}_e\). If the member carries no distributed or other element load, recover the six local end actions directly from \(\{q'\}_e=[K']_e\{u'\}_e\) (written \(\{f'\}=[K']^{(e)}\{u'\}\) in the code and earlier sections) — axial force, shear, and moment at each end. If the member does carry a distributed or other element load, its local nodal-load vector \(\{p'\}_e\) was already added into \(\{F\}\) exactly once in step 5, so the raw stiffness product alone is not the end-action vector; instead recover it as \(\{q'\}_e=[K']_e\{u'\}_e-\{p'\}_e\), subtracting \(\{p'\}_e\) exactly once (§3, “Looking Ahead”). In either case \(\{q'\}_e\) follows the node-on-element convention fixed in §2.6; check the results, where possible, against an independent statics free-body solution.

Appendix — The \(6\times6\) Global Frame Stiffness Matrix

The boxed matrix in §1.4 was obtained by the same triple product as the truss case, just carried out on \(6\times6\) matrices instead of \(4\times4\) ones. Splitting \([K']^{(e)}\) and \([T]\) into node-\(i\)/node-\(j\) quadrants,

\[ [K']^{(e)} = \begin{bmatrix}[k_{ii}'] & [k_{ij}']\\ [k_{ji}'] & [k_{jj}']\end{bmatrix}, \qquad [T] = \begin{bmatrix}[R] & [0]\\ [0] & [R]\end{bmatrix} \]

the block structure of the product collapses to four \(3\times3\) blocks,

\[ [K]^{(e)} = [T]^T[K']^{(e)}[T] = \begin{bmatrix} [R]^T[k_{ii}'][R] & [R]^T[k_{ij}'][R]\\ [R]^T[k_{ji}'][R] & [R]^T[k_{jj}'][R] \end{bmatrix} \]

so the whole derivation reduces to four \(3\times3\) sandwiches with the same \([R]\) instead of one \(6\times6\) sandwich with a full \([T]\) — exactly the block-diagonal shortcut promised in §1.3. Carrying out one representative block, \([R]^T[k_{ii}'][R]\), with

\[ [k_{ii}'] = \begin{bmatrix}k_a & 0 & 0\\ 0 & b & d\\ 0 & d & \tfrac{4EI}{L}\end{bmatrix}, \qquad [R]=\begin{bmatrix}c&s&0\\-s&c&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad b=\tfrac{12EI}{L^3},\ d=\tfrac{6EI}{L^2} \]

gives, after multiplying out,

\[ [R]^T[k_{ii}'][R] = \begin{bmatrix} k_ac^2+bs^2 & (k_a-b)cs & -ds\\ (k_a-b)cs & k_as^2+bc^2 & dc\\ -ds & dc & \tfrac{4EI}{L} \end{bmatrix} \]

which is exactly the top-left \(3\times3\) quadrant of the boxed matrix in §1.4 (with \(a\) written there for \(k_a\)). The remaining three quadrants follow the same pattern — \([k_{jj}']\) sandwiched the same way reproduces the bottom-right block with matching signs, and the two off-diagonal blocks \([k_{ij}']=[k_{ji}']^T\) pick up the sign flips already visible in the boxed matrix. As in the 2D truss lecture's appendix, omitting the bending terms (\(b=d=0\), i.e. \(I=0\)) collapses this block straight back down to the \(2\times2\) truss rotation sandwich \(k_a\begin{bmatrix}c^2&cs\\cs&s^2\end{bmatrix}\) from the 2D truss lecture — the same algebraic consistency check noted in §1.4, now visible one block at a time; as there, this is a limit of the translational stiffness, not a claim that a real \(I=0\) element is a valid frame model.
