Lecture 2A — Interpolation

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Construct 1D linear and quadratic interpolating functions from a set of data points.
  • Recognize the piecewise "hat function" representation of a global piecewise-linear interpolant.
  • Write and apply the general Lagrange interpolation formula, and connect it to hat functions as a special case.
  • Extend 1D interpolation to 2D via bilinear and biquadratic interpolation on a rectangular grid.
  • Explain why this matters: these same interpolation (shape) functions are what the finite element method uses to approximate geometry and field variables inside an element.

Scope

Terminology

A quick terminology note before we begin: interpolation matches specified nodal values exactly, while approximation may instead seek the best fit according to some chosen criterion and need not match every data point exactly. This lecture is concerned almost entirely with the former — nodal interpolation — which is what the finite element method builds on directly.

1. 1D Linear Interpolation

1.1 Concept

Given two known points $(x_k, y_k)$ and $(x_{k+1}, y_{k+1})$, linear interpolation represents the interpolated field between them by the unique straight line passing through the two nodal values:

$$ y(x) = y_k\,\frac{x - x_{k+1}}{x_k - x_{k+1}} + y_{k+1}\,\frac{x - x_k}{x_{k+1} - x_k} $$

Each coefficient multiplying $y_k$ or $y_{k+1}$ is a shape function (or interpolation function): it equals $1$ at its own node and $0$ at the other node.

1.2 Normalized (Natural) Coordinates

The formula above is correct but ties the shape functions to the specific global locations $x_k$ and $x_{k+1}$. It is far more convenient — and this is the convention finite element codes actually use — to introduce a normalized (or "natural") coordinate $\xi$ that maps every segment onto the same fixed reference interval, regardless of where that segment sits in $x$. The standard convention is to normalize to $\xi \in [-1, 1]$, with $\xi=-1$ at node $x_k$ and $\xi=+1$ at node $x_{k+1}$:

$$ \xi = \frac{2x - x_k - x_{k+1}}{x_{k+1} - x_k} $$

In terms of $\xi$, the two linear shape functions take on a strikingly simple, segment-independent form:

$$ N_1(\xi) = \frac{1-\xi}{2}, \qquad N_2(\xi) = \frac{1+\xi}{2} $$

Here local node $1$ corresponds to the global node $x_k$ and local node $2$ corresponds to $x_{k+1}$ — using local labels $1,2$ rather than $k,k+1$ keeps these reference-element functions visually distinct from the global hat functions $h_k(x)$ of Section 1.3.

The interpolant then becomes $y(\xi) = N_1(\xi)\,y_k + N_2(\xi)\,y_{k+1}$. Each $N$ still equals $1$ at its own node ($\xi=-1$ or $\xi=+1$) and $0$ at the other — the defining shape-function property is unchanged — but the formula itself no longer depends on the segment's actual endpoints at all. Every linear segment in the mesh, no matter its length or location, is interpolated with the same pair of functions once it is mapped into $\xi$. This is exactly the idea Section 4 reuses to build the 2D bilinear element on a normalized $(\xi,\eta)$ square instead of an arbitrary rectangle.

Worked example — normalized linear interpolation

Reuse the segment $x_k=0.2,\ x_{k+1}=0.6$ with $y_k=2,\ y_{k+1}=-1$ from Section 1.4, and find $y$ at $x=0.5$.

Map $x=0.5$ into the normalized coordinate first:

$$ \xi = \frac{2(0.5) - 0.2 - 0.6}{0.6-0.2} = \frac{1-0.8}{0.4} = \frac{0.2}{0.4} = 0.5 $$

Then evaluate the two shape functions at $\xi=0.5$ and blend the nodal values:

$$ N_1(0.5) = \frac{1-0.5}{2} = 0.25, \qquad N_2(0.5) = \frac{1+0.5}{2} = 0.75 $$

$$ y(0.5) = (0.25)(2) + (0.75)(-1) = 0.5 - 0.75 = \boxed{-0.25} $$

Check against the direct (non-normalized) formula from Section 1.1: $y(0.5) = 2\cdot\frac{0.5-0.6}{0.2-0.6} + (-1)\cdot\frac{0.5-0.2}{0.6-0.2} = 2(0.25) + (-1)(0.75) = 0.5-0.75 = -0.25$ ✓ — both routes agree, exactly as they must.

Worked example — normalized quadratic interpolation

The same $\xi\in[-1,1]$ idea extends to the three-point quadratic case of Section 2. These basis functions follow directly from the quadratic Lagrange construction in Section 3.3 after choosing nodes at $\xi=-1,0,1$:

$$ N_{-1}(\xi) = \frac{\xi(\xi-1)}{2}, \qquad N_{0}(\xi) = 1-\xi^2, \qquad N_{1}(\xi) = \frac{\xi(\xi+1)}{2} $$

Using the data from Section 2.2 ($F=2,-1,3$ at the three nodes, now relabeled by natural position rather than by $x$-value) and evaluating at the midpoint $\xi=0$:

$$ F(0) = N_{-1}(0)(2) + N_0(0)(-1) + N_1(0)(3) = (0)(2) + (1)(-1) + (0)(3) = \boxed{-1} $$

which correctly reproduces the middle nodal value $F(x_1)=-1$ — exactly what a shape function evaluated at its own node must do.

1.3 Piecewise (hat) functions

When data spans more than two points $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n$, we interpolate piecewise, one segment at a time. Define the hat function $h_k(x)$ associated with node $x_k$:

$$ h_k(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x - x_{k-1}}{x_k - x_{k-1}}, & x_{k-1} \le x \le x_k \\ \dfrac{x - x_{k+1}}{x_k - x_{k+1}}, & x_k \le x \le x_{k+1} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$

This definition applies to the interior nodes $k=1,\dots,n-1$. For the endpoint nodes, $h_0$ and $h_n$ have support on only one adjacent segment, since there is no $x_{-1}$ or $x_{n+1}$ to define a second piece.

The hat functions have the defining property $h_k(x_k) = 1$ and $h_k(x_j) = 0$ for $j \neq k$. The global piecewise-linear interpolant is then a single clean sum over all nodes:

$$ y(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} y_k\, h_k(x) $$

Partition of unity. At every $x$, the hat functions touching that point always sum to exactly $1$: $\sum_k h_k(x) = 1$. This is easy to overlook but important — it guarantees that a constant field ($y_k = c$ for every node) is reproduced exactly, since $y(x) = c\sum_k h_k(x) = c$. The same property holds for the general Lagrange basis functions introduced in Section 3. In finite elements, partition of unity is also one of the key ingredients needed for physically meaningful interpolation and element consistency.

Proof, on a single element: in the normalized coordinate $\xi$ from Section 1.2, $N_1(\xi) + N_2(\xi) = \dfrac{1-\xi}{2} + \dfrac{1+\xi}{2} = 1$. Since only two hat functions are ever nonzero inside any interior element — the ones belonging to that element's two endpoints — this local identity is exactly $\sum_k h_k(x) = 1$ restricted to that element, so the global piecewise-linear basis sums to $1$ everywhere, not just at the nodes.

Note — connecting notation: A hat function is assembled from local degree-one Lagrange basis functions on the elements adjacent to node $k$. It is therefore the piecewise-linear, globally continuous version of the local Lagrange interpolation basis: on the left segment it equals the local basis function associated with the right-hand node, and on the right segment it equals the local basis function associated with the left-hand node. Written in the normalized coordinate $\xi$ from Section 1.2, these local linear bases take the familiar forms $N_1(\xi)=(1-\xi)/2$ and $N_2(\xi)=(1+\xi)/2$. The term "hat function" simply describes the resulting piecewise-linear shape; it is the same interpolation idea developed more generally in Section 3.

1.4 Worked Example

Interpolate the following data with piecewise linear segments:

$x$ 0.2 0.6 0.8
$F(x)$ 2 −1 3

Segment 1 ($0.2 \le x \le 0.6$):

$$ g(x) = 2\cdot\frac{x-0.6}{0.2-0.6} + (-1)\cdot\frac{x-0.2}{0.6-0.2} $$

$$ \boxed{g(x) = -7.5x + 3.5}, \quad 0.2 \le x \le 0.6 $$

Check: $g(0.2) = 2$ ✓, $\; g(0.6) = -1$ ✓

Segment 2 ($0.6 \le x \le 0.8$):

$$ g(x) = -1\cdot\frac{x-0.8}{0.6-0.8} + 3\cdot\frac{x-0.6}{0.8-0.6} $$

$$ \boxed{g(x) = 20x - 13}, \quad 0.6 \le x \le 0.8 $$

Check: $g(0.6) = -1$ ✓, $\; g(0.8) = 3$ ✓

2. 1D Quadratic Interpolation

2.1 Concept

Quadratic interpolation fits a parabola through three points at once rather than a line through two. It's worth distinguishing three related but different ideas that this label can refer to: a single quadratic passed through exactly three nodes (the case treated here), piecewise quadratic interpolation over a mesh of three-node elements (used later in the finite element context), and a global quadratic fit when only three nodes happen to be available for the whole domain. In this section, we use the first meaning: given three distinct nodes $x_0,x_1,x_2$, construct the unique second-order polynomial that passes through all three nodal values. Piecewise quadratic elements are deferred to the FEM discussion.

2.2 Worked Example

Fit a single quadratic through:

$x$ 0.2 0.6 0.8
$F(x)$ 2 −1 3

Using the Lagrange quadratic basis (derived in Section 3.3) with $x_0=0.2,\ x_1=0.6,\ x_2=0.8$:

$$ g(x) = 2\,L_{0,2}(x) + (-1)\,L_{1,2}(x) + 3\,L_{2,2}(x) $$

Expanding and collecting terms:

$$ \boxed{g(x) = \frac{275x^2 - 265x + 54}{6} \;\approx\; 45.83x^2 - 44.17x + 9} $$

Check: $g(0.2) = 2$ ✓, $\; g(0.6) = -1$ ✓, $\; g(0.8) = 3$ ✓

Notice this single quadratic passes through all three points exactly — unlike the piecewise-linear case in Section 1.4, which needed two separate line segments.

3. Lagrange Interpolation — General Formulation

3.1 General definition

The Lagrange form of the interpolating polynomial generalizes both examples above to any order $n$. For $k = 0, 1, \dots, n$, define:

$$ L_{k,n}(x) = \prod_{\substack{j=0 \ j \neq k}}^{n} \frac{x - x_j}{x_k - x_j}, \qquad n \ge 1, \qquad L_{0,0}(x) = 1 $$

so that the interpolating polynomial through $n+1$ points is:

$$ P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} L_{k,n}(x)\, P(x_k) $$

Each $L_{k,n}(x)$ satisfies the same defining property as the hat functions in Section 1.3:

$$ L_{k,n}(x_j) = \delta_{kj} \quad \text{(1 at its own node, 0 at all others)} $$

The hat function and the degree-one Lagrange basis share the same nodal interpolation property. More precisely, a global hat function is assembled from degree-one local Lagrange basis functions on the elements adjacent to its associated node.

Uniqueness. Given $n+1$ distinct nodes $x_0,\dots,x_n$, there is exactly one polynomial of degree $\le n$ passing through all $n+1$ nodal values — the Lagrange form above is simply one convenient way to write that unique polynomial down. This is worth stating explicitly now, because later, when hierarchical or Hermite-type bases are introduced, it is this same uniqueness result (not the specific formula) that keeps getting reused.

Feature Piecewise-linear hats Single Lagrange polynomial
Nodes used Two per element All selected nodes
Support Local (compact, two segments) Usually global
Polynomial degree One, on each element Up to $n$ over the full interval
Continuity Continuous across nodes ($C^0$) Globally polynomial (smooth everywhere)
FEM relevance Standard nodal basis Local Lagrange polynomials are used on individual elements; assembling them across elements produces the FEM approximation

Common pitfall. Two mistakes show up constantly when students first build these products by hand:

  • Mixing up node order in the product. Each factor in $L_{k,n}(x)$ must skip $j=k$ — forgetting to omit that term (or omitting the wrong one) silently produces a function that is no longer $1$ at its own node.
  • Treating local support as global. A hat function $h_k(x)$ is nonzero only on the two segments touching node $x_k$ — it is locally supported. The Lagrange polynomial $L_{k,n}(x)$ built from all $n+1$ nodes at once, by contrast, is generally nonzero across the entire domain. Piecewise-linear interpolation stitches together many small, local Lagrange problems; a single higher-order Lagrange fit is one global one. Confusing the two is a frequent source of error when a fit produces unexpected oscillation between distant nodes.

3.2 Lagrange Linear Interpolation ($n=1$)

$$ L_{0,1}(x) = \frac{x - x_1}{x_0 - x_1}, \qquad L_{1,1}(x) = \frac{x - x_0}{x_1 - x_0} $$

$$ P(x) = L_{0,1}(x)\,P(x_0) + L_{1,1}(x)\,P(x_1) $$

This reproduces the linear interpolation formula from Section 1.1 exactly.

3.3 Lagrange Quadratic Interpolation ($n=2$)

$$ L_{0,2}(x) = \frac{(x-x_1)(x-x_2)}{(x_0-x_1)(x_0-x_2)}, \qquad L_{1,2}(x) = \frac{(x-x_0)(x-x_2)}{(x_1-x_0)(x_1-x_2)}, \qquad L_{2,2}(x) = \frac{(x-x_0)(x-x_1)}{(x_2-x_0)(x_2-x_1)} $$

$$ P(x) = L_{0,2}(x)\,P(x_0) + L_{1,2}(x)\,P(x_1) + L_{2,2}(x)\,P(x_2) $$

These are the basis functions used directly in the worked example of Section 2.2.

4. 2D Bilinear Interpolation

4.1 Concept

Bilinear interpolation extends linear interpolation to functions of two variables $(x, y)$ on a rectilinear grid. It is performed by applying 1D linear interpolation first in one direction, then again in the other.

4.2 Derivation

Interpolate in $x$ along each of the two bounding $y$-rows $y_1$ and $y_2$ (with $x_1 \le x \le x_2$ and $y_1 \le y \le y_2$):

$$ f(x, y_1) \approx f(x_1,y_1)\,\frac{x_2 - x}{x_2-x_1} + f(x_2,y_1)\,\frac{x - x_1}{x_2-x_1} $$

$$ f(x, y_2) \approx f(x_1,y_2)\,\frac{x_2 - x}{x_2-x_1} + f(x_2,y_2)\,\frac{x - x_1}{x_2-x_1} $$

Then interpolate the two results in $y$:

$$ f(x,y) \approx f(x,y_1)\,\frac{y_2-y}{y_2-y_1} + f(x,y_2)\,\frac{y-y_1}{y_2-y_1} $$

These three steps are algebraically correct but easy to sign-error by hand or in code, because the fractions mix $x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2$ in varying positions. It is far cleaner to first change variables to normalized (natural) coordinates $\xi,\eta$, using exactly the $[-1,1]$ convention introduced for the 1D case in Section 1.2:

$$ \xi = \frac{2x - x_1 - x_2}{x_2 - x_1}, \qquad \eta = \frac{2y - y_1 - y_2}{y_2 - y_1} $$

so that $\xi=-1$ at $x_1$ and $\xi=+1$ at $x_2$ (and likewise for $\eta$ and $y$). Substituting and simplifying, the three interpolation steps above collapse into one clean weighted sum of the four corner values, each weight being a product of a 1D linear shape function in $\xi$ (Section 1.2) and a 1D linear shape function in $\eta$ — exactly the "linear $\times$ linear" tensor-product structure the name bilinear promises:

$$ \boxed{f(\xi,\eta) \approx f_{11}\,\frac{(1-\xi)(1-\eta)}{4} + f_{21}\,\frac{(1+\xi)(1-\eta)}{4} + f_{12}\,\frac{(1-\xi)(1+\eta)}{4} + f_{22}\,\frac{(1+\xi)(1+\eta)}{4}} $$

where $f_{11}=f(x_1,y_1)$, $f_{21}=f(x_2,y_1)$, $f_{12}=f(x_1,y_2)$, and $f_{22}=f(x_2,y_2)$ are the four corner values of the rectangle, sitting respectively at $(\xi,\eta)=(-1,-1),\,(1,-1),\,(-1,1),\,(1,1)$. Each of the four coefficients is $1$ at its own corner and $0$ at the other three — the same defining property every shape function in this lecture shares — and, as with the hat functions in Section 1.3, the four coefficients sum to $1$ for any $(\xi,\eta)$ (partition of unity). The symbol $\approx$ emphasizes that the interpolant matches the nodal data exactly but need not equal the underlying field at interior points.

4.3 Worked Example

Given the corner data:

$x$ $y$ $f(x,y)$
2 0 16
2 8 −24
6 0 8
6 8 0

Find $f(5, 2)$, with $x_1=2,\ x_2=6,\ y_1=0,\ y_2=8$, so $f_{11}=16,\ f_{21}=8,\ f_{12}=-24,\ f_{22}=0$.

First convert to normalized coordinates, using the same $[-1,1]$ mapping as Section 1.2:

$$ \xi = \frac{2(5) - 2 - 6}{6-2} = \frac{2}{4} = 0.5, \qquad \eta = \frac{2(2) - 0 - 8}{8-0} = \frac{-4}{8} = -0.5 $$

Then apply the boxed formula from Section 4.2 directly:

$$ f(5,2) \approx 16\cdot\frac{(1-0.5)(1-(-0.5))}{4} + 8\cdot\frac{(1+0.5)(1-(-0.5))}{4} + (-24)\cdot\frac{(1-0.5)(1+(-0.5))}{4} + 0\cdot\frac{(1+0.5)(1+(-0.5))}{4} $$

$$ f(5,2) \approx 16(0.1875) + 8(0.5625) - 24(0.0625) + 0(0.1875) $$

$$ \boxed{f(5,2) \approx 3 + 4.5 - 1.5 + 0 = 6} $$

Check: at each corner ($\xi,\eta \in \{-1,1\}$) the formula returns exactly that corner's value — try $\xi=-1,\eta=-1$ and confirm it returns $f_{11}=16$.

5. Extending to 2D Biquadratic Interpolation

The same tensor-product idea that turned two 1D linear bases into the bilinear rectangle in Section 4 generalizes directly to quadratic basis functions. A 9-node biquadratic quadrilateral reference element has three nodes along each local axis $\xi$ and $\eta$, normalized the same way as every other natural coordinate in this lecture: $\xi,\eta\in[-1,1]$, with the two edge nodes at $\xi=-1$ and $\xi=1$ and the mid-side node at $\xi=0$ (and likewise for $\eta$). Every one of the 9 shape functions is simply the product of one 1D basis function per direction:

$$ N_{pq}(\xi,\eta) = N_p(\xi) \cdot N_q(\eta), \qquad p,q \in \{-1,0,1\} $$

To see where this comes from rather than just stating it, write out the three 1D quadratic bases explicitly for a local axis with nodes at $\xi=-1$ (edge), $\xi=0$ (mid), $\xi=1$ (edge), using the general Lagrange formula from Section 3.3:

$$ N_{-1}(\xi) = \frac{\xi(\xi-1)}{2}, \qquad N_{0}(\xi) = 1-\xi^2, \qquad N_{1}(\xi) = \frac{\xi(\xi+1)}{2} $$

and identically for $N_{-1}(\eta), N_0(\eta), N_1(\eta)$ (these are exactly the bases used in the normalized quadratic example of Section 1.2). The corner node at $(\xi,\eta)=(-1,-1)$ then combines the two "edge" bases — one per direction:

$$ N_{\text{corner}}(\xi,\eta) = N_{-1}(\xi)\cdot N_{-1}(\eta) = \frac{\xi(\xi-1)}{2}\cdot\frac{\eta(\eta-1)}{2} = \frac{\xi\eta(\xi-1)(\eta-1)}{4} $$

a mid-side node, say at $(\xi,\eta)=(0,-1)$, instead combines a "mid" basis with an "edge" basis:

$$ N_{\text{mid-side}}(\xi,\eta) = N_0(\xi)\cdot N_{-1}(\eta) = (1-\xi^2)\cdot\frac{\eta(\eta-1)}{2} $$

and the single center node combines the two "mid" bases: $N_{\text{center}}(\xi,\eta) = N_0(\xi)\cdot N_0(\eta) = (1-\xi^2)(1-\eta^2)$. Exactly as bilinear interpolation was "linear × linear," biquadratic interpolation is simply "quadratic × quadratic" — the same Lagrange machinery from Section 3, applied once per coordinate direction, on the same $[-1,1]$ natural coordinates used everywhere else in this lecture. The full 9-function set and its role in the isoparametric map are developed further in Lecture 11.

6. From Interpolation to Finite Element Shape Functions

Every formula in this lecture — hat functions, Lagrange bases, bilinear and biquadratic products — is doing the same job: build a function that is $1$ at one node and $0$ at every other node, then use it to blend nodal values smoothly across a domain.

In the finite element method, these interpolation functions are renamed shape functions, $[N]$, and used for two purposes at once inside each element:

  1. To define the element's geometry from its nodal coordinates.
  2. To approximate the displacement (or other field variable) from its nodal values.

When the same family of shape functions, expressed in natural coordinates, is used to interpolate both the element's geometry and its field variables, the element is called isoparametric — the subject of the next lecture. The emphasis is on the coordinate mapping itself, not merely on reusing similarly shaped formulas. The interpolation ideas are the same; the next lecture adds the geometric mapping and derivative transformations needed to use them in finite elements.

Summary

  • Linear and quadratic interpolation are two ends of the same idea: fit a low-order polynomial exactly through known nodal values.
  • A hat function is the globally assembled, piecewise-linear form of local degree-one Lagrange basis functions; the Lagrange formulation generalizes this nodal interpolation idea to arbitrary polynomial degree.
  • Bilinear and biquadratic interpolation are simply 1D interpolation applied independently in each coordinate direction, then combined as a tensor product.
  • These same functions reappear, unchanged in spirit, as the shape functions that define geometry and displacement fields in finite elements — the topic of the next lecture.
