Learning Objectives

  • §1 — From the strong form to minimum potential energy: review the strong-form (direct) solution of a 1D axial bar boundary value problem, then derive the principle of minimum potential energy from that same strong form and understand what makes a displacement field admissible.
  • §2 — Energy derivation of the frame element: apply that same energy principle to a real finite element, deriving the $6\times6$ 2D frame (beam–column) stiffness matrix from kinematic assumptions, shape-function interpolation, and Hooke's Law — and see why its axial and bending behavior are uncoupled; build energy-consistent equivalent nodal load vectors for forces applied within an element rather than exactly at its nodes.
  • §3 — The Rayleigh–Ritz method: learn the three steps of the Rayleigh–Ritz method for approximating the minimization of $\Pi$ over a small, well-chosen trial space, then apply it to a worked example and compare the approximate solution against the exact one.
Notation used throughout these notes

$\delta(\cdot)$ denotes a first variation (an infinitesimal, admissible change in a function). $\partial(\cdot)/\partial x$ denotes an ordinary partial derivative. $d(\cdot)/dx$ denotes an ordinary total derivative.

These three are easy to conflate — the most common bookkeeping mistake in this derivation is writing $\partial u$ when $\delta u$ is meant. Keeping them visually distinct matters especially in §3.1, where $\partial \Pi/\partial a_i$ reappears as a genuine partial derivative once $\Pi$ becomes a function of ordinary numbers $a_i$ rather than a functional of $u(x)$ — and again in Section 2 (§2.6), where the same stationarity argument, $\partial \Pi/\partial u_i = 0$, is applied to the nodal displacements $u_i$ of the frame element instead.

1. From the Strong Form to the Principle of Minimum Potential Energy

1.1 The One-Dimensional Axial Bar Problem

Sign convention

Fix this once, here, so it never has to be re-derived later: the displacement $u(x)$ is measured positive in the $+x$ direction, but both the distributed load $q$ and the end load $P$ are defined to act in the $-x$ direction. That mismatch is deliberate — it is what produces the "$+$" signs (instead of the more familiar "$-$") in the external-work and potential-energy expressions of §1.3.5–1.3.6 and §3.1.2. Whenever a sign in this lecture looks odd, check this convention first.

1.1.1 Geometry, Loading, and Boundary Conditions

Consider a bar fixed at $x=0$ and free at $x=L$, loaded by a distributed force per unit volume $q$ and a concentrated load $P$ applied at $x=L$ (both acting in the $-x$ direction).

  • $A$ = cross-sectional area
  • $q$ = force per unit volume
  • $E$ = Young's modulus
  • $P$ = applied load at $x=L$

Find: $u(x)$ (displacement), $\sigma(x)$ (stress), $\varepsilon(x)$ (strain).

1.1.2 Strain–Displacement Relation

For a finite element of length $\Delta x$,

$$\varepsilon(x) = \frac{\Delta \text{Length}}{\text{Length}} = \frac{u(x+\Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x}.$$

Taking the limit as the element shrinks to infinitesimal length $dx$:

$$\varepsilon(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{u(x+\Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = \frac{du(x)}{dx}. \tag{1.1}$$

1.1.3 Equilibrium Equation

Summing forces on an infinitesimal slice of thickness $dx$:

$$A\left(\sigma + \frac{d\sigma}{dx}dx\right) - A\sigma - qA\,dx = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{d\sigma(x)}{dx} = q. \tag{1.2}$$

1.1.4 Strong Form of the Boundary-Value Problem

RelationEquation
Equilibrium$\dfrac{d\sigma(x)}{dx} = q$
Strain–displacement$\varepsilon(x) = \dfrac{du(x)}{dx}$
Constitutive law$\sigma(x) = E\,\varepsilon(x)$
Natural (force) BC$\sigma(L)\,A = -P$
Essential (geometric) BC$u(0) = 0$

Combining the first three rows gives the governing differential equation:

$$EA\,\frac{d^2u(x)}{dx^2} = q. \tag{1.3}$$

This strong form must hold at every point $x \in (0,L)$. Later, the essential BC will be built into our trial functions by hand, while the natural BC will fall out automatically from the energy principle.

1.2 Direct (Classical) Solution of the BVP

Integrating Eq. (1.2):

$$\begin{align} \sigma(x) &= qx + C, \\ \sigma(L)A &= (qL+C)A = -P \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; C = -\frac{P}{A} - qL, \\ \sigma(x) &= q(x-L) - \frac{P}{A}. \tag{2.1} \end{align}$$

Using the constitutive law, $\varepsilon(x) = \sigma(x)/E$:

$$\varepsilon(x) = \frac{q(x-L)}{E} - \frac{P}{AE}. \tag{2.2}$$

Integrating the strain–displacement relation and applying $u(0)=0$:

$$u(x) = \int \varepsilon(x)\,dx = \frac{q}{2E}x^2 - \left(\frac{qL}{E} + \frac{P}{AE}\right)x. \tag{2.3}$$

Equations (2.1)–(2.3) are the exact solution — our benchmark for §3.2.

1.3 The Potential-Energy Formulation

1.3.1 Definition of Total Potential Energy

The total potential energy $\Pi$ is the sum of internal strain energy $U$ and the potential energy of the external forces $\Omega$:

$$\Pi = U + \Omega, \qquad \Omega = -W \;\Longrightarrow\; \Pi = U - W,$$

where $W$ is the work done by the external forces as the body deforms.

1.3.2 Admissible Displacement Fields

Definition

A kinematically admissible displacement field satisfies the essential (geometric) boundary conditions of the problem, and is differentiable enough times that the strain and stress fields associated with it can be computed.

An admissible displacement is not necessarily the actual displacement — it is merely a possible one. For our bar, admissible means only $u(0)=0$; nothing is required at $x=L$, since that boundary condition is natural, not essential.

Key idea used repeatedly below: if $u(x)$ is admissible, and $\delta u \equiv v(x)$ is any field with $\delta u(0) = 0$, then $u + \delta u$ is also admissible. $\delta u$ is called a variation of $u$.

1.3.3 First Variation of a Functional

Let $F$ be a real-valued functional of $u(x)$. Its first variation is

$$\delta F \equiv \lim_{\gamma \to 0} \frac{F(u+\gamma\,\delta u) - F(u)}{\gamma}. \tag{3.1}$$

with sum and product rules $\delta(F+Q) = \delta F + \delta Q$ and $\delta(FQ) = (\delta F)Q + (\delta Q)F$.

1.3.4 Variation of the Strain Energy

$$U = \int_0^L \frac{1}{2}\sigma\varepsilon A\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EA\left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)^2 dx. \tag{3.2}$$

Applying the definition of the first variation and simplifying:

$$\delta U = EA\int_0^L \frac{du}{dx}\frac{d(\delta u)}{dx}\,dx.$$

Integrating by parts ($t=du/dx$, $s=\delta u$):

$$\delta U = EA\,\frac{du}{dx}\,\delta u\Big|_0^L - EA\int_0^L \frac{d^2u}{dx^2}\,\delta u\,dx.$$

Since $\delta u(0)=0$, the boundary term reduces to its value at $x=L$:

$$\delta U = EA\,\frac{du}{dx}\Big|_{x=L}\delta u(L) \;-\; EA\int_0^L \frac{d^2u}{dx^2}\,\delta u\,dx. \tag{3.3}$$

1.3.5 Variation of the External Work

Because $q$ and $P$ act in $-x$ while $u$ is measured positive in $+x$:

$$W = \int_0^L (-q)\,u\,A\,dx + (-P)\,u(L).$$

This is linear in $u$, so the variation is immediate:

$$\delta W = -\int_0^L qA\,\delta u\,dx - P\,\delta u(L). \tag{3.4}$$

1.3.6 Stationarity of the Total Potential Energy

Using $\Pi = U - W$ together with (3.3) and (3.4):

$$\delta \Pi = \delta U - \delta W = \left(EA\,\frac{du}{dx}\Big|_{x=L} + P\right)\delta u(L) \;-\; \int_0^L \left(EA\,\frac{d^2u}{dx^2} - qA\right)\delta u\,dx. \tag{3.5}$$

1.3.7 The Principle of Minimum Potential Energy

Principle

Among all admissible displacements $u$ that a body can have, the one that minimizes the total potential energy of the body satisfies the strong formulation of the problem (equilibrium, strain–displacement compatibility, the constitutive law, and the boundary conditions).

Formally, $\delta \Pi = 0$ for every admissible variation $\delta u$. Because $\delta u$ is arbitrary inside $(0,L)$ and at $x=L$, Eq. (3.5) can only vanish for all such $\delta u$ if:

$$\begin{align} EA\,\frac{d^2u}{dx^2} - qA &= 0 \quad \text{for } 0 < x < L, \tag{3.6} \\ EA\,\frac{du}{dx}\Big|_{x=L} + P &= 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \sigma(L)A = -P. \tag{3.7} \end{align}$$

Equation (3.6) is exactly the governing equation (1.3), and (3.7) is exactly the natural boundary condition. The natural boundary condition, never built in by hand, emerged automatically from minimizing $\Pi$. This is why the Rayleigh–Ritz method only ever needs trial functions that satisfy the essential boundary condition.

Common pitfalls
  • Writing $\partial u$ instead of $\delta u$. $\delta u$ is a variation — an arbitrary admissible change in the trial function itself, not a partial derivative of $u$ with respect to some coordinate. Treating $\delta$ like $\partial/\partial x$ is a category error that breaks the whole argument.
  • Forgetting the boundary term at $x=L$. Integration by parts in §1.3.4 produces a boundary term evaluated at both ends; dropping the $x=L$ piece (the one that survives, since $\delta u(0)=0$) silently discards the natural boundary condition (3.7) before it has a chance to emerge.
  • Using a non-admissible trial function. A trial or variation function must satisfy the essential (displacement) boundary condition exactly — $u(0)=0$ or $\delta u(0)=0$ here — and be continuous enough for the energy integral to make sense. A function that violates $u(0)=0$, or that is discontinuous, is not admissible, no matter how good an approximation it otherwise looks like (see the counterexamples in §1.3.2).

2. Energy Derivation of the Two-Dimensional Frame Element

Why is this here?

Having established the principle of minimum potential energy for a simple 1D bar in §1, we now apply that exact same principle to derive a complete finite element from scratch. Every ingredient is one we already used in §1.3 — kinematics, a constitutive law, strain energy, and stationarity of $\Pi$ — just applied to a richer displacement field (axial stretch and bending). The payoff: the 2D frame (beam–column) element's $6\times6$ stiffness matrix, which Lecture 06B handed you ready-made for direct-stiffness assembly, is not a separate rule to memorize. It is a direct, inevitable consequence of the energy principle — this section proves the "why" behind that element, while Lecture 06B emphasizes the "how to use it."

The derivation is self-contained: starting from a single set of kinematic assumptions, it builds up through displacement interpolation, Hooke's Law (uniaxial, Poisson's effect ignored), and the strain-energy principle, straight through to the final $6\times6$ local stiffness matrix. It does not assume you've read Lecture 06B, though the result matches it exactly. Keep an eye out, too, for the specific interpolation functions chosen in §2.1 below — they are nothing more than a particular, well-chosen set of trial functions, the same kind of object that §3's Rayleigh–Ritz method builds an entire approximate solution method around.

2.1 Kinematic Assumptions and Displacement Interpolation

Consider a straight, prismatic member lying along a local \(x\)-axis, \(0\le x\le L\), with cross-sectional area \(A\) and moment of inertia \(I\) about its centroidal (neutral) axis. Let \(y\) measure distance from that centroidal axis within the cross-section.

The member deforms in two independent ways: it stretches along its axis, and it bends transversely. We describe both with a single assumption — the Euler–Bernoulli assumption: plane cross-sections remain plane and remain perpendicular to the deformed centroidal axis. Under this assumption, a fiber located a distance \(y\) from the centroidal axis has axial displacement

\[ u(x,y) = u_0(x) - y\,\theta(x), \qquad \theta(x) = \frac{dv}{dx} \]

where \(u_0(x)\) is the displacement of the centroidal axis itself (the "axial" or "membrane" displacement) and \(v(x)\) is the transverse (bending) displacement of the centroidal axis, common to every fiber in the cross-section. The \(-y\,\theta(x)\) term is exactly the extra stretch or shortening a fiber picks up by being offset from the axis about which the section rotates — the same geometric idea that makes the outside of a bent object stretch and the inside compress.

2.1.1 Nodal degrees of freedom

The member connects two nodes, 1 (at \(x=0\)) and 2 (at \(x=L\)), each carrying three nodal values that will serve as the unknowns of the finite element approximation:

\[ u_1=u_0(0),\ v_1=v(0),\ \theta_1=v'(0), \qquad u_2=u_0(L),\ v_2=v(L),\ \theta_2=v'(L) \]

Six nodal values in total — exactly the six DOFs of the frame element introduced in Lecture 06B, §1.1.

2.1.2 Axial displacement field \(u_0(x)\)

With only two nodal values (\(u_1,u_2\)) available to fix it, the simplest polynomial that can be made to satisfy both is linear — exactly the rod/truss interpolation of Lecture 04:

\[ u_0(x) = c_1+c_2x, \qquad u_0(0)=u_1,\ u_0(L)=u_2 \ \Longrightarrow\ u_0(x) = \underbrace{\Big(1-\frac{x}{L}\Big)}_{N_1^u(x)}u_1 + \underbrace{\frac{x}{L}}_{N_2^u(x)}u_2 \]

Notice what just happened: $u_0(x)=N_1^u(x)u_1+N_2^u(x)u_2$ is exactly a two-term expansion $w(x)=\sum_i a_i\varphi_i(x)$ of the kind §3.1 builds the Rayleigh–Ritz method around — only here the coefficients $a_i$ are the physically meaningful nodal displacements $u_1,u_2$, and the trial functions $\varphi_i$ are the shape functions $N_i^u$, chosen once and for all so each is $1$ at its own node and $0$ at the other. Same object, different name.

2.1.3 Transverse displacement field \(v(x)\)

The bending displacement has four nodal values to satisfy (\(v_1,\theta_1,v_2,\theta_2\)), so it needs a richer polynomial. A cubic has exactly four free coefficients:

\[ v(x) = a_1+a_2x+a_3x^2+a_4x^3 \]

Matching \(v(0)=v_1,\ v'(0)=\theta_1,\ v(L)=v_2,\ v'(L)=\theta_2\) and solving the resulting \(4\times4\) system for \(a_1,\ldots,a_4\) (the same algebra carried out in Lecture 06B, §1.2) gives

\[ v(x) = N_1^v(x)\,v_1+N_2^v(x)\,\theta_1+N_3^v(x)\,v_2+N_4^v(x)\,\theta_2 \]

with the Hermite cubic shape functions

\[ N_1^v=\frac{L^3-3Lx^2+2x^3}{L^3},\quad N_2^v=x-\frac{2x^2}{L}+\frac{x^3}{L^2}, \quad N_3^v=\frac{x^2(3L-2x)}{L^3},\quad N_4^v=\frac{x^2(x-L)}{L^2} \]

Notice the pattern across §2.1.2 and §2.1.3: in both cases, the number of polynomial terms was chosen to exactly match the number of nodal values being enforced — two terms for two values (axial), four terms for four values (bending, since matching a slope as well as a value at each end doubles the count). This is the general rule for building interpolation functions in FEM, independent of which physical field is being interpolated.

2.2 Strain from the Displacement Field

The only strain component the Euler–Bernoulli assumption tracks is the axial (longitudinal) strain of each fiber, \(\varepsilon_x = \partial u/\partial x\). Differentiating the displacement field of §2.1 with respect to \(x\) (holding \(y\) fixed, since we're following one specific fiber along the member's length):

\[ \varepsilon_x(x,y) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{du_0}{dx} - y\,\frac{d^2v}{dx^2} \]

The first term, \(du_0/dx\), is the uniform axial strain familiar from Lecture 04. The second term, \(-y\,v''(x)\), is the extra strain a fiber picks up purely from being at offset \(y\) while the section rotates — it is positive (tension) on one side of the axis and negative (compression) on the other, exactly the classical picture of a bent beam's cross-section.

2.3 Constitutive Relation (Hooke’s Law)

Elementary beam theory treats each longitudinal fiber as being in a state of pure uniaxial stress: \(\sigma_y=\sigma_z=0\), and the only stress component that matters for the member's resistance is \(\sigma_x\). Hooke's Law for this uniaxial state is simply

\[ \sigma_x = E\,\varepsilon_x \]

with no Poisson ratio \(\nu\) appearing anywhere. (A fiber under uniaxial stress does, physically, also contract or expand laterally by \(-\nu\varepsilon_x\) in the \(y\) and \(z\) directions — but those lateral strains do no work against \(\sigma_x\) and never enter the beam's strain energy, so ignoring them, as elementary beam theory always does, changes nothing about the stiffness matrix that follows.) Substituting the strain from §2.2:

\[ \sigma_x(x,y) = E\left(\frac{du_0}{dx} - y\,\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}\right) \]

2.4 Strain Energy of the Frame Member

For a linearly elastic body, the internal strain energy stored is

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}\int_V \sigma_x\,\varepsilon_x\, dV = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L\!\!\int_A E\left(\frac{du_0}{dx}-y\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}\right)^2 dA\,dx \]

Expanding the square (the cross-sectional integral only touches the \(y\)-dependence, since \(du_0/dx\) and \(v''\) depend only on \(x\) and come out of the \(\int_A(\cdot)\,dA\)):

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L E\left[ \left(\frac{du_0}{dx}\right)^2\!\!\int_A dA \;-\;2\frac{du_0}{dx}\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}\!\int_A y\,dA \;+\;\left(\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}\right)^2\!\!\int_A y^2\,dA \right] dx \]

Because \(y\) is measured from the centroidal axis, the three cross-sectional integrals are, by definition,

\[ \int_A dA = A, \qquad \int_A y\,dA = 0, \qquad \int_A y^2\,dA = I \]

The middle term — the one that would couple axial stretching and bending — vanishes identically because \(\int_A y\,dA=0\) is exactly the definition of the centroid. This is the rigorous reason the axial and bending behaviors are uncoupled in the local stiffness matrix; it isn't an extra assumption layered on top of Euler–Bernoulli theory, it falls straight out of integrating over a cross-section measured from its own centroid. What remains is

\[ \boxed{\,U = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EA\left(\frac{du_0}{dx}\right)^2 dx \;+\;\frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EI\left(\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}\right)^2 dx\,} \]

two separate, additive energy terms: axial strain energy (depending only on \(u_1,u_2\)) and bending strain energy (depending only on \(v_1,\theta_1,v_2,\theta_2\)).

2.5 Discretization with Shape Functions

Differentiating the interpolations of §2.1.2 and §2.1.3:

\[ \frac{du_0}{dx} = \Big[-\tfrac1L\ \ \tfrac1L\Big]\begin{Bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{Bmatrix} = [B_a]\{u_a\}, \qquad \frac{d^2v}{dx^2} = \big[N_1^{v\prime\prime}\ N_2^{v\prime\prime}\ N_3^{v\prime\prime}\ N_4^{v\prime\prime}\big] \begin{Bmatrix}v_1\\\theta_1\\v_2\\\theta_2\end{Bmatrix} = [B_b]\{u_b\} \]

where, differentiating the Hermite functions of §2.1.3 twice,

\[ N_1^{v\prime\prime}=-\frac{6}{L^2}+\frac{12x}{L^3},\quad N_2^{v\prime\prime}=-\frac{4}{L}+\frac{6x}{L^2},\quad N_3^{v\prime\prime}=\frac{6}{L^2}-\frac{12x}{L^3},\quad N_4^{v\prime\prime}=-\frac{2}{L}+\frac{6x}{L^2} \]

Substituting into the boxed energy of §2.4:

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}\{u_a\}^T\left(\int_0^L EA\,[B_a]^T[B_a]\,dx\right)\{u_a\} \;+\; \frac{1}{2}\{u_b\}^T\left(\int_0^L EI\,[B_b]^T[B_b]\,dx\right)\{u_b\} \]

Each parenthesized quantity is a symmetric matrix built purely from the shape-function derivatives — and, by the definition of the element stiffness matrix as the object making \(U=\tfrac12\{u\}^T[K]\{u\}\) true, each one is a stiffness matrix: \([k_a]=\int_0^L EA[B_a]^T[B_a]\,dx\) for the axial DOFs, and \([k_b]=\int_0^L EI[B_b]^T[B_b]\,dx\) for the bending DOFs.

2.6 Stationarity Condition and Nodal Forces

The step that turns a strain-energy expression into a usable stiffness relation is the principle of minimum total potential energy. The total potential energy of the element is the strain energy stored minus the work done by the nodal forces moving through the nodal displacements,

\[ \Pi(\{u\}) = U(\{u\}) - \{u\}^T\{f\} \]

Equilibrium corresponds to \(\Pi\) being stationary with respect to every nodal DOF, \(\partial\Pi/\partial u_i = 0\) for each \(i\). Since \(U\) is the quadratic form \(U=\tfrac12\{u\}^T[K]\{u\}\) built in §2.5, and \([K]\) is symmetric, \(\partial U/\partial\{u\} = [K]\{u\}\) (the same identity used any time a quadratic form is differentiated), so stationarity gives

\[ \frac{\partial \Pi}{\partial\{u\}} = [K]\{u\} - \{f\} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\{f\} = [K]\{u\}} \]

which is exactly the stiffness relation the direct stiffness method has been using all along, now obtained from an energy stationarity argument rather than assumed outright. This is the formal sense in which "\(K_{ij}\) is the force at DOF \(i\) caused by a unit displacement at DOF \(j\)": \(K_{ij}=\partial^2U/\partial u_i\partial u_j\), the Hessian of the strain energy.

2.7 Evaluation of the Element Integrals

Axial block. \([B_a]=[-1/L,\ 1/L]\) is constant along the member, so the integral is trivial:

\[ [k_a] = EA\int_0^L [B_a]^T[B_a]\,dx = EA\cdot L\cdot\frac{1}{L^2} \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} = \frac{EA}{L}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} \]

— exactly the rod stiffness matrix of Lecture 04.

Bending block. Each entry \((k_b)_{ij}=EI\int_0^L N_i^{v\prime\prime}N_j^{v\prime\prime}\,dx\) is a short polynomial integral (each \(N_i^{v\prime\prime}\) is linear in \(x\), so every integrand is at most quadratic). Carrying out all ten distinct products gives

\[ [k_b] = \frac{EI}{L^3}\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 6L & -12 & 6L\\ 6L & 4L^2 & -6L & 2L^2\\ -12 & -6L & 12 & -6L\\ 6L & 2L^2 & -6L & 4L^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

— the same Euler–Bernoulli beam bending matrix used in Lecture 06B.

2.8 The Local \(6\times6\) Frame Stiffness Matrix

Since §2.4 showed the strain energy is the exact sum of an axial term (depending only on \(u_1,u_2\)) and a bending term (depending only on \(v_1,\theta_1,v_2,\theta_2\)), with no cross term, the two blocks assembled in §2.7 simply occupy their own rows/columns in the full \(6\times6\) local stiffness matrix, in the DOF order \((u_1,v_1,\theta_1,u_2,v_2,\theta_2)\), with zeros everywhere else:

\[ [K'] = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0 & -\dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0\\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & 0 & -\dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2}\\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{4EI}{L} & 0 & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{2EI}{L}\\[6pt] -\dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0 & \dfrac{EA}{L} & 0 & 0\\[6pt] 0 & -\dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & 0 & \dfrac{12EI}{L^3} & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2}\\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{2EI}{L} & 0 & -\dfrac{6EI}{L^2} & \dfrac{4EI}{L} \end{bmatrix} \]

This is precisely the local frame element stiffness matrix used throughout Lecture 06B — but note everything that had to be assumed there (the block structure, the specific bending matrix, the fact that axial and bending don't interact locally) has, in this derivation, been proven from three ingredients: an interpolated displacement field consistent with the number of nodal DOFs, uniaxial Hooke's Law with Poisson's effect ignored, and the principle of minimum potential energy applied to the resulting strain energy.

2.9 Equivalent Nodal Loads for Distributed and Concentrated Forces

Everything above assumed the only forces acting on the element were the nodal forces \(\{f\}\) themselves, so the external work was simply \(\{u\}^T\{f\}\). In practice, loads often act within the element rather than exactly at a node — a point load partway along a beam, or a distributed load such as self-weight, wind pressure, or a uniform pressure. The same shape functions used to build \([K]\) let us convert any such load into an equivalent (or "consistent") nodal load vector \(\{f_{eq}\}\), by insisting that it do the same virtual work through the assumed displacement field as the real, distributed load does.

2.9.1 Work-equivalence principle

Let \(q(x)\) be a real transverse load (force per unit length) acting along the member, and let \(\{\delta u_b\}=\{\delta v_1,\delta\theta_1,\delta v_2,\delta\theta_2\}\) be an arbitrary virtual nodal displacement, producing the virtual displacement field \(\delta v(x)=[N(x)]\{\delta u_b\}\) through the same Hermite shape functions \([N]=[N_1^v,N_2^v,N_3^v,N_4^v]\) used in §2.1.3. The virtual work done by the real distributed load as it rides along this virtual field is

\[ \delta W = \int_0^L q(x)\,\delta v(x)\,dx = \int_0^L q(x)\,[N(x)]\,dx\ \{\delta u_b\} = \{\delta u_b\}^T\!\int_0^L [N(x)]^T q(x)\,dx \]

Equating this to the virtual work an equivalent nodal load vector \(\{f_{eq}\}\) would do, \(\{\delta u_b\}^T\{f_{eq}\}\), for every possible \(\{\delta u_b\}\) (the same "arbitrary virtual displacement" logic used throughout virtual-work methods) forces

\[ \boxed{\ \{f_{eq}\} = \int_0^L [N(x)]^T\,q(x)\,dx\ } \]

Notice this is exactly the same sandwiching operation used to build \([k_b]\) in §2.7 — there, two copies of a shape-function derivative were sandwiched around \(EI\); here, one copy of the shape function itself is "sandwiched" around the applied load \(q(x)\). Same recipe, one fewer shape function. A concentrated force \(P\) at \(x=a\) is just the limiting case \(q(x)=P\,\delta(x-a)\) (Dirac delta), which collapses the integral to the shape functions evaluated at that one point:

\[ \{f_{eq}\} = P\,[N(a)]^T = P\,\big\{N_1^v(a),\ N_2^v(a),\ N_3^v(a),\ N_4^v(a)\big\}^T \]

Both cases requested below are special cases of the boxed formula above — one with \(q(x)\) a Dirac delta, one with \(q(x)\) constant.

2.9.2 Concentrated interior force

Let a transverse point load \(P\) act at \(x=a\), measured from node 1, with \(0<a<L\); write \(b=L-a\) for its distance from node 2. Evaluating each Hermite shape function of §2.1.3 at \(x=a\) and substituting \(b=L-a\) gives the classical "fixed-end" equivalent load vector:

\[ F_1 = \frac{Pb^2(3a+b)}{L^3}, \qquad M_1 = \frac{Pab^2}{L^2}, \qquad F_2 = \frac{Pa^2(3b+a)}{L^3}, \qquad M_2 = -\frac{Pa^2b}{L^2} \]

so that, in the full six-entry local force vector (with the axial DOFs untouched, since this load has no axial component),

\[ \{f_{eq}'\} = \Big\{0,\ \ \tfrac{Pb^2(3a+b)}{L^3},\ \ \tfrac{Pab^2}{L^2},\ \ 0,\ \ \tfrac{Pa^2(3b+a)}{L^3},\ \ -\tfrac{Pa^2b}{L^2}\Big\}^T \]

Two checks confirm this is not just energy-consistent but also physically sensible:

  • Force equilibrium: \(F_1+F_2 = P\) exactly — the two end shears alone add up to the entire applied load, independent of where along the span it acts.
  • Moment equilibrium about node 1: \(M_1+M_2+F_2L = Pa\) exactly — the equivalent load vector produces the same net moment about node 1 as the real point load does. (I verified both identities symbolically.)

Showing the moment check explicitly: substituting the boxed expressions for $M_1$, $M_2$, and $F_2$ above,

$$ M_1+M_2+F_2L = \frac{Pab^2}{L^2} - \frac{Pa^2b}{L^2} + \frac{Pa^2(3b+a)}{L^3}\,L = \frac{Pab^2}{L^2} - \frac{Pa^2b}{L^2} + \frac{Pa^2(3b+a)}{L^2}. $$

Collecting terms over the common denominator $L^2$ and using $b=L-a$:

$$ M_1+M_2+F_2L = \frac{P}{L^2}\Big[ab^2 - a^2b + 3a^2b + a^3\Big] = \frac{P}{L^2}\Big[ab^2 + 2a^2b + a^3\Big] = \frac{Pa}{L^2}\big(b^2+2ab+a^2\big) = \frac{Pa}{L^2}(a+b)^2 = \frac{Pa}{L^2}\,L^2 = Pa, $$

which is exactly the moment the real point load $P$ produces about node 1 — confirming the check stated above.

So the "energy-consistent" load vector derived from pure virtual work turns out to be statically equivalent to the original point load as well — not a coincidence: virtual work is preserved in particular for rigid-body virtual displacements, and rigid-body equilibrium is exactly what force/moment balance checks.

Special case — load at midspan (\(a=b=L/2\)): the formulas above reduce to \(F_1=F_2=P/2\) and \(M_1=-M_2=PL/8\), the familiar fixed-end values for a center point load quoted in most structural analysis texts.

2.9.3 Uniformly distributed load

Let a transverse load of constant intensity \(w\) (force per unit length) act over the full length of the member, \(q(x)=w\). Substituting into the boxed formula of §2.9.1 and carrying out the four integrals (each is a short polynomial integral, exactly like the ones in §2.7):

\[ F_1=\int_0^L w\,N_1^v\,dx=\frac{wL}{2},\qquad M_1=\int_0^L w\,N_2^v\,dx=\frac{wL^2}{12} \]

\[ F_2=\int_0^L w\,N_3^v\,dx=\frac{wL}{2},\qquad M_2=\int_0^L w\,N_4^v\,dx=-\frac{wL^2}{12} \]

so that

\[ \{f_{eq}'\} = \Big\{0,\ \ \tfrac{wL}{2},\ \ \tfrac{wL^2}{12},\ \ 0,\ \ \tfrac{wL}{2},\ \ -\tfrac{wL^2}{12}\Big\}^T \]

The two shear terms split the total load \(wL\) evenly between the two nodes, exactly as elementary statics would suggest; the two moment terms are the classical fixed-end moments for a uniformly loaded span, equal in magnitude and opposite in algebraic sign because of the node-1/node-2 sign convention established in Lecture 06B's alternative (equilibrium-based) derivation. As a sanity check, this is precisely the limiting case of §2.9.2's point-load formula integrated continuously over \(0\le a\le L\) with \(P\to w\,da\) — the two derivations are consistent with each other, as they must be.

2.9.4 Remarks on axial loads and global assembly

The identical logic applies to a load component along the member's own axis, using the linear shape functions \(N_1^u,N_2^u\) of §2.1.2 in place of the Hermite functions — and because those shape functions are linear, the results are simpler: a uniformly distributed axial load \(q_a\) splits evenly, \(\{q_aL/2,\ q_aL/2\}\), and an axial point load \(P\) at \(x=a\) splits by the simple lever rule, \(\{P(1-a/L),\ Pa/L\}\) — the same rule used to distribute a load between two supports in elementary statics.

Finally, \(\{f_{eq}'\}\) as constructed above lives in the member's local coordinates. Before it can be added into the structure's global load vector, it must be rotated the same way any other local force vector is, using the transpose of the transformation matrix from Lecture 06B, §1.3:

\[ \{f_{eq}\} = [T]^T\{f_{eq}'\} \]

and then assembled into the global \(\{F\}\) vector at the DOF indices of that member's two nodes — exactly the same assembly bookkeeping used for the stiffness matrix in §2.8, just applied to a load vector instead of a stiffness matrix.

3. The Rayleigh–Ritz Method and a Worked Example

3.1 The Three Steps of the Rayleigh–Ritz Method

The exact strong-form solution required solving a differential equation directly — fine for this bar, but intractable for most real geometries and loadings. The Rayleigh–Ritz method instead finds an admissible displacement field that approximately minimizes $\Pi$, searching only a small, well-chosen family of functions rather than all possible functions. Recall: "admissible" means only $u(0)=0$ — nothing more.

3.1.1 Choose an Admissible Trial Function

$$w(x) = a_0 \varphi_0(x) + a_1 \varphi_1(x) + a_2 \varphi_2(x) + \dots \tag{5.1}$$

where $\varphi_0, \varphi_1, \dots$ are chosen, linearly independent, admissible functions (each with $\varphi_i(0)=0$), and $a_0, a_1, \dots$ are constants to be determined.

3.1.2 Form the Potential Energy in Terms of the Unknown Coefficients

$$\Pi(u) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EA\left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)^2 dx + \int_0^L qA\,u(x)\,dx + P\,u(L).$$

Signs, revisited: both terms carry a $+$ here, not a $-$, because §1.3.5 defined $q$ and $P$ as acting in $-x$, which puts a minus sign into $W$ itself ($W = \int(-q)uA\,dx + (-P)u(L)$). Combined with $\Pi = U - W$, the two negatives cancel, leaving the external-work terms added rather than subtracted. This is the same $\Pi$ as Eq. (3.5)–(3.7), just with $u$ left symbolic instead of already varied.

Substituting $u \approx w$ turns $\Pi$ from a functional of $u(x)$ into an ordinary multivariable function of $a_0, a_1, \dots$:

$$\Pi(a_0, a_1, \dots) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L EA\left(a_0\varphi_0' + a_1\varphi_1'+\dots\right)^2 dx + \int_0^L qA\left(a_0\varphi_0+a_1\varphi_1+\dots\right)dx + P\left(a_0\varphi_0(L)+a_1\varphi_1(L)+\dots\right). \tag{5.2}$$

3.1.3 Minimize With Respect to Each Coefficient

$$\frac{\partial \Pi(a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots)}{\partial a_i} = 0, \qquad i = 0, 1, 2, \dots \tag{5.3}$$

This gives one algebraic equation per unknown coefficient. Solving for $a_0, a_1, \dots$ gives the approximate solution $u(x) \approx \sum_i a_i \varphi_i(x)$.

Rayleigh–Ritz method, in one box
  1. Assume $w(x)=\sum_i a_i \varphi_i(x)$ with each $\varphi_i$ admissible.
  2. Substitute $w$ into $\Pi$ to get an ordinary function $\Pi(a_0,a_1,\dots)$.
  3. Minimize: solve $\partial \Pi/\partial a_i = 0$ for every $i$.

The more (independent, admissible) terms $\varphi_i$ used, the closer $\Pi$ gets to its true minimum — and the closer $w(x)$ gets to the exact solution $u(x)$.

3.2 Worked Example: Axially Loaded Elastic Bar

Take $q=P=E=L=A=1$. From Eq. (2.3), the exact solution is

$$u(x) = \frac{x^2}{2} - 2x,$$

and substituting into $\Pi$ gives the exact minimum potential energy:

$$\Pi_{\text{exact}} = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 (x-2)^2\,dx + \int_0^1 \left(\frac{x^2}{2}-2x\right)dx + \frac{1}{2} - 2 = -1.16667.$$

We approach this value from above using progressively richer Ritz trial functions — since $\Pi_{\text{exact}}$ is the true minimum, every admissible trial gives $\Pi \geq \Pi_{\text{exact}}$.

3.2.1 One-Term Polynomial Trial Function

$$\Pi(b) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 b^2\,dx + \int_0^1 (bx)\,dx + b = \frac{b^2}{2} + \frac{3}{2}b.$$
$$\frac{d\Pi}{db} = b + \frac{3}{2} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; b = -\frac{3}{2}, \qquad \Pi = -1.125.$$

3.2.2 Two-Term (Polynomial + Trigonometric) Trial Function

The second term $a\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2}x)$ is added — rather than, say, $ax^2$ — because it is linearly independent of $bx$ and admissible ($\varphi(0)=0$), and because $\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2}x)$ already curves away from the straight line $bx$ in exactly the interior region ($0

$$\Pi(a,b) = a + \frac{3}{2}b + ab + \frac{b^2}{2} + \frac{2a}{\pi} + \frac{a^2\pi^2}{16}.$$
$$\frac{\partial \Pi}{\partial a} = 0:\;\; 1 + b + \frac{2}{\pi} + \frac{a\pi^2}{8} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial \Pi}{\partial b} = 0:\;\; \frac{3}{2} + a + b = 0.$$
$$a = \frac{4(\pi-4)}{\pi(\pi^2-8)} \approx -0.5846, \qquad b = -\frac{3}{2}-a \approx -0.9154, \qquad \Pi = -1.16493.$$

Why $\Pi$ only ever goes down

Notice in Fig. 6.2 that both Ritz values of $\Pi$ sit above the exact value, and that adding the second term brought $\Pi$ closer to (not past) the exact minimum. This is not a coincidence: restricting the admissible space to a finite-dimensional subspace can only make it harder to minimize $\Pi$, never easier, so the Ritz method always produces an upper bound on the true minimum potential energy (and, up to a sign, on the true strain energy). Enlarging the trial space — adding more terms, as in §3.2.2 — can only bring $\Pi$ down toward the exact value, never below it. The “Error vs. exact” column of the comparison table in §3.2.3 above is exactly this gap, and it shrinks monotonically as richer trial spaces are used — the same guarantee that makes mesh refinement in the finite element method reliably improve, rather than degrade, the solution.

4. Summary and Key Takeaways

  • The strong form of a 1D bar problem can be solved directly by integration, but that becomes impractical for complex geometries and loadings.
  • The principle of minimum potential energy is an equivalent formulation: the true displacement field is the admissible one that minimizes $\Pi = U-W$. Minimizing $\Pi$ over all admissible fields recovers the strong form exactly, including the natural boundary condition, which was never built in by hand.
  • That same principle is not just a 1D abstraction: applied to the 2D frame (beam–column) element in Section 2, the identical kinematics → interpolation → constitutive law → energy → stationarity chain produces the exact $6\times6$ local stiffness matrix used in direct-stiffness assembly, plus a systematic rule (§2.9) for converting loads applied within an element — not at its nodes — into energy-consistent equivalent nodal loads.
  • The Rayleigh–Ritz method (§3.1) approximates the minimization of $\Pi$ over a small family of admissible trial functions, turning an infinite-dimensional minimization into a finite-dimensional one: assemble $\Pi(a_0,a_1,\dots)$, then solve $\partial \Pi/\partial a_i = 0$.
  • Richer trial spaces give monotonically better approximations, as the worked example in §3.2 demonstrated.

The derivation of the 2D frame element in Section 2 can be summarized by the following chain of reasoning:

  1. Kinematics: Euler–Bernoulli assumption → displacement of any fiber is \(u_0(x)-y\theta(x)\), with \(\theta=v'\).
  2. Interpolation: match the number of polynomial terms to the number of nodal values — linear for \(u_0\) (2 DOFs), Hermite cubic for \(v\) (4 DOFs).
  3. Constitutive law: uniaxial Hooke's Law \(\sigma_x=E\varepsilon_x\), Poisson's effect ignored since the lateral strains it would produce do no work against \(\sigma_x\).
  4. Energy: integrate \(\tfrac12\sigma_x\varepsilon_x\) over the volume; the cross term vanishes because \(y\) is measured from the centroid, leaving separate axial and bending energy terms.
  5. Stationarity: minimum potential energy turns the quadratic energy form into \(\{f\}=[K]\{u\}\), with \([K]\) the Hessian of the strain energy — reproducing the frame element matrix of Lecture 06B exactly, and mirroring the same $\delta\Pi=0$ argument used for the continuous bar in §1.3.
  6. Loads not applied at nodes: the same shape functions convert a concentrated interior force or a distributed load into an energy-consistent equivalent nodal load vector, \(\{f_{eq}\}=\int_0^L[N]^Tq(x)\,dx\), which turns out to also be statically equivalent to the original load (§2.9).
Where this is going: from Rayleigh–Ritz to the Finite Element Method

The finite element method is precisely the Rayleigh–Ritz method with local, compactly supported trial functions in place of global ones.

The trial functions $\varphi_i(x)$ used in §3.1 were global functions (a line, a sine curve) spanning the whole bar, while the frame element of Section 2 used piecewise Hermite and linear shape functions local to a single member. Look again at those shape functions with that distinction in mind:

  • The linear axial shape function from §2.1.2, $N_1^u(x)=1-x/L$, is exactly a simple, local, piecewise linear trial function: it equals $1$ at node 1, falls off linearly to $0$ at node 2, and is understood to be identically zero everywhere outside that one element.
  • The Hermite functions $N_1^v,N_2^v,N_3^v,N_4^v$ from §2.1.3 are, in exactly the same way, local piecewise cubic trial functions — each nonzero only over the length of a single frame member and zero everywhere else.

The Ritz expansion $w(x)=\sum_i a_i\varphi_i(x)$ of Eq. (5.1) becomes the finite element method the moment the $\varphi_i(x)$ are chosen to be these simple, local, element-level shape functions $N_j$ rather than global functions spanning the whole domain. Each $N_j$ then "lives" only within the one or two elements that share its node, and is exactly zero elsewhere — exactly the kind of function already used to build the frame element. The unknown Ritz coefficients $a_i$ become the nodal displacements $u_i$ (or $u_1,v_1,\theta_1,\dots$ for the frame), and minimizing $\Pi$ by solving $\partial \Pi/\partial a_i=0$, Eq. (5.3), is exactly the stationarity condition $\partial \Pi/\partial u_i=0$ of §2.6 — the same operation, applied element by element and summed over the whole structure, that assembles the global stiffness matrix $[K]$ and solves $\{f\}=[K]\{u\}$.

That single change — trading global trial functions for local, piecewise ones — is what makes the method practical for arbitrarily complex geometries, and is the subject of the next lecture.
