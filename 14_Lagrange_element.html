Lecture 10: The Lagrange Element

Learning Objectives

  • Build the one-dimensional quadratic Lagrange polynomials $L_{-1},L_0,L_{+1}$ and multiply them into the nine tensor-product shape functions $N_i(\xi,\eta)=L_{\xi_i}(\xi)\,L_{\eta_i}(\eta)$ of the 9-node Lagrange element
  • Use the same $N_i$ for geometry and displacements (isoparametric) and explain why the element sides become parabolas and where the mid-side nodes may sit
  • Form the $2\times9$ shape-derivative matrix, the Jacobian $[J]$ and $[J]^{-1}$, and explain why $\det[J]$ is now a bicubic polynomial that must be checked over the whole element, not only at the corners
  • Assemble $[B(\xi,\eta)]=[B_1][B_2][B_3]$ with $[B_3]$ of size $4\times18$
  • Write $[K^e]$ ($18\times18$) as a $3\times3$ Gauss quadrature sum and explain why the $2\times2$ rule leaves three spurious zero-energy modes
  • Say what the extra nodes buy: exact pure bending and curved boundaries, at the price of a larger matrix

What is reused and what is new

  • Reused, not re-derived: $[D]$ from Lecture 6, the pipeline $\{\varepsilon\}=[B]\{u^e\}$, $[K^e]=\int_{V^e}[B]^T[D][B]\,dV$ from Lecture 7, and the one-dimensional Lagrange interpolation of Lecture 2A.

1. Picking Up From Lecture 9

Lecture 9 gave us a four-node quadrilateral of any straight-sided shape. Two limitations remain:

  • Straight sides. A curved boundary (a hole, a fillet, an arch) can only be approximated by a polygon of straight element sides.
  • Linear displacement along each side. Along $\eta=\pm1$ the displacement is a straight line between two nodes. A beam in bending needs the cross-section to curve, and the four-node element cannot do it without producing spurious shear strain (the “shear locking” of Lecture 9).

The size of the problem is easy to measure. Take one rectangular element, load it with the exact pure-bending displacement field $u=\kappa xy,\;v=-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(x^2+\nu y^2)$ at its nodes, and compare the strain energy the element stores with the exact value (plane stress, $\nu=0.3$; half-width $a$, half-height $b$):

Shape of the element4-node element ($2\times2$ rule): energy / exact9-node Lagrange element ($3\times3$ rule): energy / exact
$a/b=1$ (square)1.4841.000
$a/b=2$2.6371.000
$a/b=4$ (slender)7.2531.000

The four-node element is too stiff by a factor that grows with the aspect ratio. The nine-node element is exact, because the bending field contains only the terms $xy,\,x^2,\,y^2$, and every one of them lies in the span of its shape functions (Section 3).

The plan in one line: raise the polynomial degree from 1 to 2 in each direction by multiplying one-dimensional Lagrange polynomials → use the same functions for geometry (curved sides) → reuse Lecture 9's pipeline (Jacobian, $[B]=[B_1][B_2][B_3]$, Gauss quadrature) with larger matrices and a higher-order rule.

2. One-Dimensional Lagrange Polynomials

Lecture 2A introduced Lagrange interpolation through given points. For nodes $s_0,s_1,\dots,s_p$ the polynomial that is 1 at node $k$ and 0 at the others is

$$ L_k(s)=\prod_{\substack{j=0\\ j\ne k}}^{p}\frac{s-s_j}{s_k-s_j},\qquad L_k(s_j)=\delta_{kj},\qquad \sum_{k=0}^{p}L_k(s)=1 . $$

Any function sampled at the nodes is interpolated by $f(s)\approx\sum_k f_k\,L_k(s)$, the unique polynomial of degree $p$ through the samples. For our element we need the case $p=2$ on the three equally spaced nodes $s=-1,\,0,\,+1$. Label each polynomial by the node where it equals one:

$$ L_{-1}(s)=\tfrac12\,s\,(s-1),\qquad L_{0}(s)=1-s^2,\qquad L_{+1}(s)=\tfrac12\,s\,(s+1) $$

and their derivatives, needed for $[B]$ and $[J]$:

$$ L_{-1}'(s)=s-\tfrac12,\qquad L_0'(s)=-2s,\qquad L_{+1}'(s)=s+\tfrac12 . $$

Three facts will carry the whole lecture: (i) the Kronecker property $L_k(s_j)=\delta_{kj}$; (ii) the partition of unity $\sum L_k=1$; (iii) the first moment $\sum_k s_k L_k(s)=s$ (a linear function is interpolated exactly, here $-L_{-1}+L_{+1}=s$).

3. The Nine Shape Functions

On the parent square $(\xi,\eta)\in[-1,1]^2$ place a $3\times3$ grid of nodes at $\xi,\eta\in\{-1,0,1\}$. We keep the numbering of Lecture 9 for the corners and add the others: corners 1–4 counterclockwise from the lower left, mid-sides 5–8 (bottom, right, top, left), and the center 9.

This is the whole construction: the shape function of a node is the product of the one-dimensional Lagrange polynomial in $\xi$ for that node's $\xi_i$ and the one in $\eta$ for its $\eta_i$. The product is 1 at its own node and 0 at the other eight, because at least one factor is zero there. Multiplying out gives one formula per node type:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{corners (}i=1..4\text{):}\quad & N_i=\tfrac14\,\xi_i\xi\;\eta_i\eta\,(1+\xi_i\xi)(1+\eta_i\eta)\\ \text{mid-sides, }\xi_i=0\text{ (}i=5,7\text{):}\quad & N_i=\tfrac12\,\eta_i\eta\,(1+\eta_i\eta)\,(1-\xi^2)\\ \text{mid-sides, }\eta_i=0\text{ (}i=6,8\text{):}\quad & N_i=\tfrac12\,\xi_i\xi\,(1+\xi_i\xi)\,(1-\eta^2)\\ \text{center (}i=9\text{):}\quad & N_9=(1-\xi^2)(1-\eta^2) \end{aligned} $$

For reference, node by node:

$$ \begin{aligned} N_1&=\tfrac14\xi\eta(\xi-1)(\eta-1), & N_2&=\tfrac14\xi\eta(\xi+1)(\eta-1), & N_3&=\tfrac14\xi\eta(\xi+1)(\eta+1), & N_4&=\tfrac14\xi\eta(\xi-1)(\eta+1),\\ N_5&=\tfrac12\eta(\eta-1)(1-\xi^2), & N_6&=\tfrac12\xi(\xi+1)(1-\eta^2), & N_7&=\tfrac12\eta(\eta+1)(1-\xi^2), & N_8&=\tfrac12\xi(\xi-1)(1-\eta^2). \end{aligned} $$

The derivatives follow from the product rule, one factor at a time, with the $L'$ formulas of Section 2:

$$ N_{i,\xi}=L'_{\xi_i}(\xi)\,L_{\eta_i}(\eta),\qquad N_{i,\eta}=L_{\xi_i}(\xi)\,L'_{\eta_i}(\eta). $$

Properties worth checking, since everything later relies on them:

  • Kronecker delta: $N_i(\xi_j,\eta_j)=\delta_{ij}$.
  • Partition of unity: $\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i=\Big(\sum_a L_a(\xi)\Big)\Big(\sum_b L_b(\eta)\Big)=1\cdot1=1$.
  • Zero-sum derivatives: $\sum_i N_{i,\xi}=\sum_i N_{i,\eta}=0$, so a rigid translation produces no strain.
  • Exact linear fields: $\sum_i\xi_iN_i=\xi$ and $\sum_i\eta_iN_i=\eta$ (first moment of Section 2, applied in each direction).
  • Completeness: the nine functions span $\{1,\xi,\eta,\xi\eta,\xi^2,\eta^2,\xi^2\eta,\xi\eta^2,\xi^2\eta^2\}$ (“biquadratic”, the space $Q_2$): a complete quadratic plus three higher terms.
  • Negative values. Unlike the bilinear functions of Lecture 9, which stay between 0 and 1, the quadratic ones dip below zero (the smallest value of each corner and mid-side function is $-\tfrac18$). A negative weight is not a bug; the nine weights still sum to 1.

The Lagrange family. Taking $p+1$ equally spaced nodes in each direction gives the Lagrange element of degree $p$ with $(p+1)^2$ nodes: $p=1$ is the four-node element of Lecture 9, $p=2$ is this lecture's nine-node element, $p=3$ has 16 nodes. The serendipity 8-node element is a cousin that drops the center node (and the $\xi^2\eta^2$ term); it is cheaper but is not a product of Lagrange polynomials. Everything below works for any $p$ with the matrix sizes and the Gauss rule changed.

4. The Isoparametric Lagrange Map

Isoparametric Lagrange element. The same nine functions $N_i(\xi,\eta)$ interpolate both the geometry and the displacement field. Geometry is therefore specified by nine nodes, not four.

$$ x(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i(\xi,\eta)\,x_i,\qquad y(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i(\xi,\eta)\,y_i,\qquad u=\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i\,u_i,\qquad v=\sum_{i=1}^{9}N_i\,v_i $$

In matrix form the displacement interpolation is $\{u,v\}^T=[N]\{u^e\}$ with $\{u^e\}=\{u_1,v_1,\dots,u_9,v_9\}^T$ (18 DOFs) and

$$ [N]=\begin{bmatrix}N_1&0&N_2&0&\cdots&N_9&0\\0&N_1&0&N_2&\cdots&0&N_9\end{bmatrix}\quad(2\times18). $$

What the mapping does. Each of the four edges $\xi=\pm1$, $\eta=\pm1$ is mapped by three nodes and a quadratic, so it becomes a parabola through the corner, mid-side, corner nodes. Lines of constant $\xi$ or $\eta$ inside the element are curves too. When the mid-side nodes sit at the midpoints of the straight lines between corners and node 9 sits at the corner average, all quadratic terms cancel and the map is exactly the bilinear map of Lecture 9: the straight-sided four-node element is a special case.

Where may the mid-side node sit?

Look at one side with end nodes at positions $x_1,x_3$ and mid-node at $x_2=\tfrac12(x_1+x_3)+\delta$, so the node is displaced by $\delta$ from the midpoint, and let $\ell=x_3-x_1$. Along the side, $x(s)=x_1L_{-1}(s)+x_2L_0(s)+x_3L_{+1}(s)$, and

$$ \frac{dx}{ds}=\frac{\ell}{2}-2\delta\,s . $$

For the map to be one-to-one along the side we need $dx/ds>0$ on $[-1,1]$, which requires $|\delta|<\ell/4$: the mid-node must stay inside the middle half of its side. At exactly $|\delta|=\ell/4$ the Jacobian vanishes at the end node; that is the “quarter-point element” used deliberately in fracture mechanics to build a $1/\sqrt r$ strain singularity, but it is a defect in an ordinary element. This $\sqrt r$ singularity is exactly the strain field near a crack tip in linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM), so the quarter-point element is used deliberately in fracture analyses even though it is invalid as a general-purpose element.

Why this choice is a good one

  • Patch-test consistency. Because $\sum N_i=1$ and $\sum N_i\xi_i=\xi$, a linear displacement field sampled at the nodes is reproduced exactly on any valid element, curved or not, so constant strain is always representable.
  • Quadratic fields. On a parallelogram $x,y$ are linear in $\xi,\eta$, so $u=$ any quadratic in $x,y$ is a quadratic in $\xi,\eta$ and lies in the span of the nine functions: reproduced exactly (this is why pure bending is exact). On a curved element $x,y$ contain $\xi^2\eta$ and $\xi\eta^2$ terms, $x^2$ leaves the span, and quadratic fields are only approximated. Convergence is preserved, the leading error just changes.
  • One code path. Only the shape functions, their derivatives and the Gauss rule differ from Lecture 9.

Terminology. If the geometry used the four corner functions only (straight sides) while the displacements used all nine, the element would be subparametric. This lecture uses the isoparametric case: nine nodes for both.

5. The Jacobian and the Chain Rule

The chain rule is unchanged from Lecture 9: for any $T(\xi,\eta)$,

$$ \begin{Bmatrix}\partial T/\partial\xi\\ \partial T/\partial\eta\end{Bmatrix}=[J]\begin{Bmatrix}\partial T/\partial x\\ \partial T/\partial y\end{Bmatrix},\qquad \begin{Bmatrix}\partial T/\partial x\\ \partial T/\partial y\end{Bmatrix}=[\Gamma]\begin{Bmatrix}\partial T/\partial\xi\\ \partial T/\partial\eta\end{Bmatrix},\quad [\Gamma]=[J]^{-1}. $$

$[J]$ is easy because $x(\xi,\eta),y(\xi,\eta)$ are known; only the sums now have nine terms:

$$ [J]=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}N_{1,\xi}&N_{2,\xi}&\cdots&N_{9,\xi}\\N_{1,\eta}&N_{2,\eta}&\cdots&N_{9,\eta}\end{bmatrix}}_{2\times9} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_1&y_1\\x_2&y_2\\\vdots&\vdots\\x_9&y_9\end{bmatrix}}_{9\times2},\qquad [J]^{-1}=\frac1{\det[J]}\begin{bmatrix}J_{22}&-J_{12}\\-J_{21}&J_{11}\end{bmatrix}. $$

What the Jacobian is, in words

Think of the Jacobian as the local distortion of the element. A tiny square patch $d\xi\times d\eta$ in the idealized parent square is stretched, sheared and rotated into a small parallelogram in the physical element. The matrix $[J]$ records that transformation: its first row is the image of the $\xi$-direction, its second row is the image of the $\eta$-direction. Its determinant $\det[J]$ is the area-scaling factor of the patch, $\det[J]\,d\xi\,d\eta=dx\,dy$. If $\det[J]>0$ the patch keeps its orientation; if $\det[J]=0$ it collapses to a line; if $\det[J]<0$ the element has folded over itself, which is geometrically impossible for a real material region.

What $\det[J]$ means. It is the local area-scaling factor of the map, so

$$dA=dx\,dy=\det[J]\,d\xi\,d\eta\quad(\text{requires }\det[J]>0),\qquad A_{\text{element}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\int_{-1}^{1}\det[J]\,d\xi\,d\eta.$$

$\det[J]$ must be strictly positive at every point of the element. A zero or negative value means the element has folded over, has a re-entrant corner, was numbered clockwise, or has a mid-node outside the middle half of its side.

Why four corners are no longer enough. Each entry of the first row of $[J]$ is of degree 1 in $\xi$ and 2 in $\eta$ (it contains $N_{i,\xi}$); each entry of the second row is of degree 2 in $\xi$ and 1 in $\eta$. The product in the determinant therefore has degree 3 in $\xi$ and 3 in $\eta$: a bicubic polynomial with up to 16 terms. For the 4-node element the $\xi\eta$ terms cancelled and $\det[J]$ was linear, so its minimum sat at a corner. Now the minimum can lie on a side or in the interior.

Example: take the rectangle with corners $(1,1),(7,1),(7,4),(1,4)$ and move node 9 from its natural position $(4,2.5)$ to $(2,1.8)$. All four corners still have $\det[J]=4.5$, yet $\det[J]=-1.5$ at node 8, the middle of the left side. The element is invalid and a corner check would not see it. Practical checks: evaluate $\det[J]$ at all nine nodes and at the Gauss points, or sample a grid as the widgets here do (an 11×11 grid, which contains all nine nodes, the $3\times3$ Gauss points, and enough interior samples to be confident that the bicubic $\det[J]$ has not dipped below zero between nodes).

Two special cases keep $\det[J]$ simple: for a parallelogram with mid-nodes at the midpoints it is constant; for straight sides with evenly spaced nodes it is linear (the four-node case). The shortcut $A=4\det[J]|_{0,0}$ holds only in these two cases; in general use $A=\iint\det[J]\,d\xi d\eta$, which the $3\times3$ Gauss rule evaluates exactly.

What makes a good Lagrange quadrilateral? $\det[J]>0$ everywhere is the hard requirement; conditioning degrades before it fails.

  • Mid-node position: keep each mid-side node in the middle half of its side, and in practice near the midpoint unless a boundary needs curvature.
  • Curvature: a side that bulges by more than about a fifth of its length is better split into two elements.
  • Convexity and angles: corner angles within roughly $45^\circ$–$135^\circ$.
  • Aspect ratio: long side / short side $\lesssim 3$ where strain gradients are large.
  • Jacobian variation: $\max\det[J]/\min\det[J]$ over the sampled element $\lesssim 3$–$5$.
  • Center node: leave it at the natural center; it has no neighbors to share with, so moving it gains nothing and risks an interior fold.

Common student mistakes with the Jacobian

  • Hiding a negative $\det[J]$ with $|\det[J]|$. Fix the numbering or geometry instead.
  • Checking only the four corners (valid for Lecture 9, not here) or only the Gauss points, which can all be positive while a node is not (the “mid-node too far” preset is positive at all nine Gauss points yet $\det[J]<0$ at corner 2).
  • Numbering nodes in the wrong order. Corners 1–4 counterclockwise, then mid-sides 5–8 on sides 1-2, 2-3, 3-4, 4-1, then 9. Swapping a mid-node with a corner makes a nonsense map.
  • Using $[J]$ where $[J]^{-1}$ belongs (or its transpose).
  • Computing $[J]$ once for a curved element. Only parallelograms have a constant $[J]$; recompute it at every Gauss point.

6. Strain–Displacement Matrix $[B]=[B_1][B_2][B_3]$

The three-step recipe of Lecture 9 is unchanged except for the size of the last factor.

Step 1 — $[B_1]$ ($3\times4$) and Step 2 — $[B_2]$ ($4\times4$): as before

$$ \begin{Bmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}\\ \varepsilon_{yy}\\ \gamma_{xy}\end{Bmatrix}= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&0&0&1\\0&1&1&0\end{bmatrix}}_{[B_1]} \begin{Bmatrix}\partial u/\partial x\\ \partial u/\partial y\\ \partial v/\partial x\\ \partial v/\partial y\end{Bmatrix},\qquad \begin{Bmatrix}\partial u/\partial x\\ \partial u/\partial y\\ \partial v/\partial x\\ \partial v/\partial y\end{Bmatrix}= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}&0&0\\\Gamma_{21}&\Gamma_{22}&0&0\\0&0&\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}\\0&0&\Gamma_{21}&\Gamma_{22}\end{bmatrix}}_{[B_2(\xi,\eta)]} \begin{Bmatrix}\partial u/\partial \xi\\ \partial u/\partial \eta\\ \partial v/\partial \xi\\ \partial v/\partial \eta\end{Bmatrix} $$

Step 3 — $[B_3]$: differentiate the shape functions ($4\times18$)

$$ \begin{Bmatrix}\partial u/\partial \xi\\ \partial u/\partial \eta\\ \partial v/\partial \xi\\ \partial v/\partial \eta\end{Bmatrix}= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} N_{1,\xi}&0&N_{2,\xi}&0&\cdots&N_{9,\xi}&0\\ N_{1,\eta}&0&N_{2,\eta}&0&\cdots&N_{9,\eta}&0\\ 0&N_{1,\xi}&0&N_{2,\xi}&\cdots&0&N_{9,\xi}\\ 0&N_{1,\eta}&0&N_{2,\eta}&\cdots&0&N_{9,\eta} \end{bmatrix}}_{[B_3(\xi,\eta)]}\{u^e\},\qquad N_{i,\xi}=L'_{\xi_i}(\xi)L_{\eta_i}(\eta),\;\; N_{i,\eta}=L_{\xi_i}(\xi)L'_{\eta_i}(\eta). $$

Putting it together

$$ \{\varepsilon\}=[B(\xi,\eta)]\{u^e\},\qquad \underbrace{[B]}_{3\times18}=\underbrace{[B_1]}_{3\times4}\;\underbrace{[B_2]}_{4\times4}\;\underbrace{[B_3]}_{4\times18} $$

Sanity check — the rectangle at its center. For half-sides $a,b$: $[\Gamma]=\mathrm{diag}(1/a,1/b)$. At $(\xi,\eta)=(0,0)$ all corner functions and $N_9$ have zero slope, and the only nonzero slopes are $N_{6,\xi}=\tfrac12,\;N_{8,\xi}=-\tfrac12,\;N_{7,\eta}=\tfrac12,\;N_{5,\eta}=-\tfrac12$. Hence

$$ \varepsilon_{xx}=\frac{u_6-u_8}{2a},\qquad \varepsilon_{yy}=\frac{v_7-v_5}{2b},\qquad \gamma_{xy}=\frac{u_7-u_5}{2b}+\frac{v_6-v_8}{2a}: $$

central differences across the element, built from the four mid-side nodes. A rigid translation gives zero because the differences cancel.

The nodal coordinates are linear functions of the nodes' own parent coordinates, and the shape functions reproduce linear functions exactly ($\sum N_i=1,\ \sum N_i\xi_i=\xi,\ \sum N_i\eta_i=\eta$), so

$$ x=\sum N_i\,(4+2\xi_i+\eta_i)=4+2\xi+\eta,\qquad y=\sum N_i\,(2+\eta_i)=2+\eta . $$

This is exactly the map of Lecture 9: with straight sides and midpoint nodes the nine-node element and the four-node element have the same geometry.

(b) Jacobian.

$$ [J]=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det[J]=2,\qquad [\Gamma]=[J]^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12&0\\-\tfrac12&1\end{bmatrix} $$

The map is affine, so $[J]$ is the same at every point, and $A=\iint\det[J]\,d\xi d\eta=2\cdot4=8\ \text{in}^2$ ✓.

(c) The three factors. $[B_1]$ and $[B_2]$ are the same as in Lecture 9, $[B_2]=\begin{bmatrix}\frac12&0&0&0\\-\frac12&1&0&0\\0&0&\frac12&0\\0&0&-\frac12&1\end{bmatrix}$; $[B_3]$ is the general $4\times18$ matrix of Section 6.

(d) The product at the center. At $(0,0)$ only nodes 5–8 have nonzero slope ($N_{6,\xi}=\tfrac12,\,N_{8,\xi}=-\tfrac12,\,N_{7,\eta}=\tfrac12,\,N_{5,\eta}=-\tfrac12$). With $\partial N/\partial x=\tfrac12N_{,\xi}$ and $\partial N/\partial y=-\tfrac12N_{,\xi}+N_{,\eta}$, and listing only the nonzero columns (all columns of nodes 1–4 and 9 are zero):

$$ 4\,[B(0,0)]=\begin{array}{c|cccccccc} & u_5&v_5&u_6&v_6&u_7&v_7&u_8&v_8\\\hline \varepsilon_{xx}&0&0&1&0&0&0&-1&0\\ \varepsilon_{yy}&0&-2&0&-1&0&2&0&1\\ \gamma_{xy}&-2&0&-1&1&2&0&1&-1 \end{array} $$

Skewing has coupled the rows exactly as in Lecture 9 ($\varepsilon_{yy}$ depends on the $u$-side through $-\tfrac12$ in $[\Gamma]$), but now with a different set of nodes: the strain at the center is a difference of mid-side values.

8. Stiffness Matrix and Gauss Quadrature

Change of variables in the integral

$$ \boxed{\;[K^e]=t\int_{-1}^{1}\!\int_{-1}^{1}[B(\xi,\eta)]^T[D]\,[B(\xi,\eta)]\;\det[J(\xi,\eta)]\;d\xi\,d\eta,\qquad \det[J]>0\;}\qquad(18\times18) $$

The plain $\det[J]$ (not $|\det[J]|$) is correct only because the map preserves orientation, $\det[J]>0$ throughout the element. For a general element $[B]$ contains $[J]^{-1}=\mathrm{adj}[J]/\det[J]$, so the integrand is rational and we integrate numerically.

Gauss quadrature

Each Gauss point contributes one $18\times18$ matrix of rank at most 3.

Why $3\times3$ is “full” for the Lagrange element. For a rectangle (or parallelogram) $[J]$ is constant, and each entry of $[B]$ is a product $L'(\xi)L(\eta)$ or $L(\xi)L'(\eta)$: degree 1 in one variable and 2 in the other. The product $[B]^T[D][B]$ then reaches degree 4 in each variable. A 2-point rule is exact only to degree 3 and misses the top terms; a 3-point rule (exact to degree 5) integrates $[K^e]$ exactly. For a curved element the integrand is rational and $3\times3$ is an accurate approximation. In general the Lagrange element of degree $p$ uses $(p+1)\times(p+1)$ points: $2\times2$ for Lecture 9's element, $3\times3$ here, $4\times4$ for 16 nodes.

Checks you should always run on a computed $[K^e]$:

  • Symmetric: $K_{ij}=K_{ji}$ ✓
  • Rigid-body translation gives zero force: each row sums to zero over its $u$ columns and over its $v$ columns ✓
  • Eigenvalues: exactly three are zero (two translations, one rotation) and fifteen are positive: $\approx 113580,\ 55039,\ 54788,\ 29781,\ 20504,\ 18199,\ 17482,\ 14103,\ 9573,\ 7028,\ 6343,\ 3807,\ 3352,\ 2019,\ 447$. So $\operatorname{rank}[K^e]=18-3=15$ ✓
  • Quadrature check: replacing the $3\times3$ rule by $4\times4$ changes no entry (to round-off), as expected for a parallelogram.

The matrix is bigger than Lecture 9's $8\times8$ for the same piece of material: it carries eighteen DOFs and a richer deformation space (15 independent deformation modes instead of 5).

Check your understanding: why did the 4-node corner test (check $\det[J]$ at four corners) have to be replaced?

Because $\det[J]$ is bicubic, not linear. A linear function attains its extremes at corners; a bicubic one can go negative on a side or in the interior while all four corners are positive (see the “center node dragged off” preset). Check at all nine nodes and the Gauss points, or sample a grid.

9. Full vs. Reduced Integration

The element has 18 DOFs and 3 rigid-body modes, so a healthy $[K^e]$ must have rank 15. Each Gauss point supplies rank at most 3.

RulePointsMaximum rankRank of $[K^e]$ (example element)Spurious zero-energy modes
Full: $3\times3$92715none
Reduced: $2\times2$412123 (hourglass modes)
Center: $1\times1$13312

Why $2\times2$ breaks down. $4\times3=12<15$, so at least three deformation patterns have zero strain at all four sampling points and store no energy. For a square element they can be written down: $u=(3\xi^2-1)(3\eta^2-1)$ with $v=0$; the same pattern in $v$; and the mixed mode $u=\xi(1-3\eta^2),\ v=-\eta(1-3\xi^2)$. Each has all its strains equal to zero at $\xi,\eta=\pm1/\sqrt3$. Try the three rules in frame 4 of the stepper: the rank and the “spurious” count change as described.

The partial sums also show how rank builds up: for the example the first six Gauss points give ranks 3, 6, 8, 11, 14 and 15, so six points are enough for rank, and the rest provide the accuracy of the integration.

Why the center node is not free. Node 9 touches only this element, so its two DOFs can be eliminated by static condensation before assembly; the element then looks, to its neighbours, like an 8-node element. Lecture 11 returns to such practical choices. For this course assume full $3\times3$ integration unless told otherwise.

Common student mistakes with integration

  • Carrying the 4-node rule over. $2\times2$ is not full integration here; the matrix comes out with rank 12 and a singular global system.
  • Wrong weights. The nine weights are products $W_iW_j$ of $5/9,8/9,5/9$ (25/81, 40/81, 64/81), not all 1.
  • Forgetting $\det[J]$ at each point, or $t$.
  • Checking validity at the Gauss points only. Nodes and sides matter too.
  • Mixing up the DOF order. It is $(u_1,v_1,\dots,u_9,v_9)$; $[B]$ columns and the rows of $[K^e]$ must follow the same order.
Check your understanding: how many Gauss points are needed, at the very least, to reach rank 15?

Five: $5\times3=15$. In practice the points overlap in the patterns they see, so the example needed six. The $2\times2$ rule has only four points ($\le12$), so it cannot succeed.

11. Algorithm and Code

Element stiffness of a 9-node Lagrange quadrilateral

  1. Input the nine nodal coordinates (corners 1–4 counterclockwise, mid-sides 5–8, center 9), $[D]$, $t$ and the Gauss rule ($3\times3$ by default).
  2. Check that $\det[J]>0$ on a grid of the parent square (including the nodes), not only at the corners.
  3. Set $[K^e]=[0]_{18\times18}$.
  4. For each Gauss point $(\xi_p,\eta_p)$ with weight $W_p$:
    1. Evaluate $N_{i,\xi}=L'_{\xi_i}(\xi)L_{\eta_i}(\eta)$ and $N_{i,\eta}=L_{\xi_i}(\xi)L'_{\eta_i}(\eta)$ ($2\times9$).
    2. Form $[J]=[N_{,\xi\eta}][X]$ and $\det[J]$.
    3. Compute $\partial N_i/\partial x,\ \partial N_i/\partial y$ from $[J]^{-1}[N_{,\xi\eta}]$ and fill $[B]$ ($3\times18$).
    4. Add $t\,W_p\,[B]^T[D][B]\det[J]$ to $[K^e]$.
  5. Return $[K^e]$; assemble with the DOF map. Check the rank (15) when you change the rule.
  6. After solving: recover $\{\sigma\}=[D][B(\xi_p,\eta_p)]\{u^e\}$ at the Gauss points.

Check your understanding: what goes wrong if mid-side node 5 is listed before corner 4?

The code assigns $(\xi_i,\eta_i)$ by position in the list. A mid-node placed in a corner slot is evaluated as though it were at a corner, so the map is scrambled and $\det[J]$ turns negative or erratic. Always follow the fixed order 1–4 corners, 5–8 mid-sides, 9 center, with each mid-side between its two corners.

12. Conclusion and Key Takeaways

  • Lagrange construction. $N_i(\xi,\eta)=L_{\xi_i}(\xi)\,L_{\eta_i}(\eta)$, a product of one-dimensional Lagrange polynomials; nine functions for $p=2$, $(p+1)^2$ in general.
  • Properties. Kronecker delta, partition of unity, completeness to $Q_2$, negative lobes allowed.
  • Isoparametric. Same $N_i$ for $x,y$ and $u,v$; sides are parabolas; mid-nodes must stay in the middle half of their sides; straight sides with midpoint nodes give back the four-node map.
  • Jacobian. $[J]=[N_{,\xi\eta}]_{2\times9}[X]_{9\times2}$; $\det[J]$ is bicubic, so validity needs a whole-element check, and the area is $\iint\det[J]$.
  • Strain matrix. $[B]=[B_1][B_2][B_3]$ with $[B_3]$ of size $4\times18$; $[B]$ is $3\times18$.
  • Stiffness. $[K^e]$ is $18\times18$, evaluated by $3\times3$ Gauss quadrature, exact for parallelograms.
  • Rank. A healthy element has rank $18-3=15$; $2\times2$ leaves 3 spurious modes, $1\times1$ leaves 12.
  • What it buys. Exact pure bending and curved boundaries, for roughly four times the number of matrix entries.
  • Looking ahead. Lecture 11 discusses the practical choices: which element, which rule, how to mesh.
